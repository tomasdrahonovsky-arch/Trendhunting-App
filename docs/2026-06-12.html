EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between authenticity and performance, heritage and reinvention. The drinks industry is caught in a moment where "selling joy" has become the strategic imperative—not shifting product, but connecting with how people feel. Mary Portas's keynote to the WSTA crystallises what's been building: consumers don't want palaces of stuff, they want sensory experiences, stories, and moments of meaning.

Three currents dominate. First, the rehabilitation of pleasure as a legitimate pursuit. From rosé diversity being celebrated rather than homogenised, to the historical debunking of pirate rum myths (they were actually moderate drinkers who invented cocktails), there's a cultural recalibration around how we frame indulgence. The "lipstick effect" framing—affordable pleasures surviving tough times—positions beverages as daily rituals rather than occasional treats.

Second, the emergence of experience-as-destination. Perelada positions itself as a "cultural destination where history, architecture, gastronomy, hospitality and wine converge." This isn't winery marketing; it's a template for how beverage brands must think about physical presence. The 127 hectares of organic vineyards become secondary to the story they enable.

Third, young people are simultaneously keeping cinema alive (78% of 16-34s are regular attendees) while losing faith in their futures. This paradox—seeking communal experiences while doubting individual outcomes—creates specific opportunities for brands that can offer belonging without demanding belief in meritocracy.

The strategic question for non-alcoholic beverages: how do you become the ritual that marks the transition between day parts, the social lubricant that doesn't require alcohol, the affordable pleasure that feels neither virtuous nor indulgent but simply good?
