## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a fundamental tension between authenticity and performance, playing out across hospitality, health, and identity. The drinks industry is witnessing a quiet revolution: 94% of alcohol-free beer drinkers also consume alcohol, confirming that moderation isn't about abstinence—it's about intentional consumption. "Zebra striping" (alternating between alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks) has moved from niche behaviour to mainstream practice, with nearly half of British pub-goers already doing it.

Simultaneously, we're seeing hospitality spaces evolve beyond transaction into transformation. From Gleneagles' Dom Pérignon cellar experience to London's Bar ALTA embracing Basque pintxos culture, the emphasis is on feeling, memory, and connection rather than mere consumption. The Macallan's Harrods boutique exemplifies this: a retail space designed around sensory immersion and personalisation, where the Six Pillars of whisky-making become architectural elements.

A counter-current runs through health and wellness discourse. The WHO's push for cancer warning labels on alcohol represents the culmination of tactics migrated from tobacco control—a regulatory approach that treats information as intervention. Meanwhile, hormone health content floods social media, raising questions about whether wellness culture empowers or pathologises women's bodies.

The World Cup is revealing how national symbols carry contested meanings. England flags trigger anxiety about political affiliation even as communities attempt to reclaim them. Scottish supporters, by contrast, are "drinking bars dry" in Boston while being praised for warmth and community contribution—a reminder that collective identity can be expressed through joyful excess rather than exclusion.

For non-alcoholic beverage brands, the opportunity lies in serving the intentional consumer who seeks both quality and flexibility, in spaces designed for lingering rather than transaction.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: Zebra Striping Goes Mainstream

**What happened:** New research from KAM Insight and Athletic Brewing reveals 94% of alcohol-free beer drinkers also consume alcohol, with 48% of British pub-goers already alternating between alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks during a single session.

**Why it is a signal:** This demolishes the binary framing of "drinker vs. non-drinker." Moderation is no longer about identity—it's about moment-to-moment choice within a single occasion.

**Societal layer:** Control / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages must be positioned as part of the drinking occasion, not separate from it. Menu placement, glassware, and pricing should signal parity with alcoholic options. The "round" is being redefined.

**What to monitor:** On-premise NA beer sales velocity during peak drinking hours; social media mentions of "pacing" or "alternating" drinks; pub menu redesigns that integrate NA options.

### SIGNAL 2: Hospitality as Memory Architecture

**What happened:** Bouchaine Vineyards hired a new DTC director specifically to create experiences that "transport people through feeling, memory and connection." Gleneagles transformed a century-old working cellar into an immersive Dom Pérignon dining space with materials sourced from Champagne's terroir.

**Why it is a signal:** Premium hospitality is shifting from service delivery to emotional engineering. The physical space becomes a vessel for narrative and sensory memory.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should invest in experience design that creates distinctive sensory memories—proprietary glassware, signature rituals, spaces that tell origin stories. The drink is the anchor; the memory is the product.

**What to monitor:** Hospitality job postings emphasising "experience design" or "guest journey"; investment in bespoke serving vessels; partnerships between beverage brands and architects/designers.

### SIGNAL 3: Third Spaces Resist Prescription

**What happened:** At an Eater-hosted discussion, hospitality operators defined third spaces by their resistance to commercial pressure: places where "lingering" is encouraged, where strangers can interact, and where outcomes are unpredictable.

**Why it is a signal:** Post-pandemic, consumers are seeking spaces that don't optimise for throughput. The value proposition is permission to exist without purchasing constantly.

**Societal layer:** Community / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages are ideal for extended stays—lower intoxication means longer, more comfortable lingering. Brands should partner with venues that explicitly welcome slow consumption and design products with extended session drinking in mind.

**What to monitor:** Venue policies on minimum spend or table time limits; emergence of "membership" or "subscription" café/bar models; urban planning discussions about public gathering spaces.

### SIGNAL 4: Lifestyle Over Terroir

**What happened:** IGP Méditerranée's UK exports grew 12% in 2025, with marketing centred on "The Med Way of Life" rather than technical winemaking credentials. The pitch is "fun, accessible and brilliant"—lifestyle first, provenance second.

**Why it is a signal:** For accessible price points, emotional association outperforms education. Consumers are buying into a feeling, not a geography lesson.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should develop clear lifestyle positioning rather than leading with process or ingredients. What mood, moment, or aspiration does the product embody? Mediterranean ease, Nordic calm, or urban energy?

**What to monitor:** Wine marketing language shifts; success of lifestyle-led beverage brands vs. ingredient-led; consumer research on purchase drivers for premium soft drinks.

### SIGNAL 5: The Tobacco Playbook Comes for Alcohol

**What happened:** Analysis of WHO's "no safe level" campaign reveals that many current alcohol policy advocates migrated from tobacco control, applying the same tactics—including cancer warning labels—to drinks.

**Why it is a signal:** Regulatory pressure on alcohol will intensify using proven frameworks. The question isn't whether labels will come, but how the industry responds.

**Societal layer:** Control / mental health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages may benefit from regulatory tailwinds as alcohol faces increasing friction. However, brands should avoid positioning as "healthy" without substantiation—the same scrutiny will eventually apply to all beverage health claims.

**What to monitor:** Legislative developments in Ireland, UK, and EU on alcohol labelling; public health funding allocations; media framing of alcohol as a public health crisis.

### SIGNAL 6: Authenticity in Production Method

**What happened:** Champagne Drappier's eighth-generation winemaker defended the saignée method for rosé, arguing that "a rosé should taste like a rosé"—prioritising flavour extraction over the pale aesthetics currently dominating the market.

**Why it is a signal:** Counter-positioning against homogenisation. As categories mature, differentiation through traditional or "authentic" methods becomes a viable strategy against trend-following.

**Societal layer:** Identity / rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands can differentiate through production transparency and heritage methods. "How it's made" becomes a story worth telling when competitors converge on similar flavour profiles.

**What to monitor:** Consumer interest in production methods; success of "craft" positioning in NA categories; backlash against "natural" or "clean" label claims.

### SIGNAL 7: Collective Joy as National Identity

**What happened:** Scottish World Cup supporters in Boston generated unprecedented beer sales, emergency deliveries, and praise from the mayor for their warmth and community contribution. The "Tartan Army" expressed identity through generous, inclusive celebration.

**Why it is a signal:** Positive collective identity can be performed through consumption rituals. The contrast with anxieties around England flags shows how the same behaviour (drinking, flag-waving) carries different cultural weight depending on perceived inclusivity.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Major sporting events are opportunities for NA brands to participate in collective celebration without the negative associations of excess. Sponsorship and activation should emphasise inclusion and extended participation.

**What to monitor:** Brand activations at World Cup 2026; social media sentiment around fan behaviour; emergence of "sober sections" or NA-focused fan zones.

### SIGNAL 8: The Landline as Status Object

**What happened:** A feature on landline adoption frames corded phones as a "post-digital flex"—a deliberate rejection of smartphone ubiquity in favour of intentional, bounded communication.

**Why it is a signal:** Digital detox is evolving from temporary retreat to permanent lifestyle choice. Objects that enforce friction become markers of sophistication.

**Societal layer:** Control / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues and brands that offer "disconnection" as a feature—phone-free spaces, analogue rituals, slow service—may attract consumers seeking respite from digital saturation. NA beverages fit naturally into mindful, present-moment experiences.

### SIGNAL 9: Hormone Discourse and Gendered Wellness

**What happened:** A Dazed piece questions whether the explosion of hormone-health content empowers women or pathologises normal bodily functions, asking if "Big Hormone" is the new wellness industrial complex.

**Why it is a signal:** Wellness culture is facing internal critique. The same audiences who embraced cycle-syncing may be tiring of constant optimisation pressure.

**Societal layer:** Mental health / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional beverages marketed around hormonal benefits should anticipate backlash. Positioning should emphasise pleasure and choice rather than correction or optimisation.

**What to monitor:** Sentiment shifts in wellness communities; regulatory scrutiny of functional claims; emergence of "anti-optimisation" wellness positioning.

### SIGNAL 10: Personalisation as Premium Marker

**What happened:** The Macallan's Harrods boutique features an engraving station for bottle personalisation, while Gleneagles' cellar experience accommodates only 12 guests with bespoke menus.

**Why it is a signal:** At the luxury tier, personalisation has shifted from nice-to-have to expected. Mass-produced products struggle to command premium pricing without customisation options.

**Societal layer:** Status / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Premium NA brands should explore personalisation—custom labels, engraving, bespoke flavour profiles, or limited-edition collaborations. The object becomes a gift or keepsake, not just a beverage.

**What to monitor:** Personalisation technology costs; consumer willingness to pay for customisation; success of limited-edition NA releases.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Zebra Menu

**Hypothesis:** Integrating NA options directly alongside alcoholic equivalents on menus (rather than in a separate section) will increase NA beverage sales by 25% and improve perceived quality scores.

**Method:** Partner with 10 on-premise venues to redesign menus with NA options placed immediately after their alcoholic counterparts (e.g., IPA followed by NA IPA). Track sales data and conduct exit surveys on perceived quality and selection satisfaction over 8 weeks. Control group maintains separate NA section.

**Success metric:** 25% increase in NA beverage sales; statistically significant improvement in quality perception scores (measured via 5-point Likert scale).

---

### Experiment 2: The Lingering License

**Hypothesis:** Venues that explicitly communicate permission to stay without continuous purchasing will see increased NA beverage sales and higher customer satisfaction scores.

**Method:** Develop "Lingering License" table cards and staff scripts for 5 café-bar venues, communicating that guests are welcome to stay and enjoy the space. Pair with a curated NA menu designed for extended sessions (lower sugar, varied flavour profiles). Measure dwell time, NA sales per visit, and NPS over 6 weeks.

**Success metric:** 15% increase in average dwell time; 20% increase in NA beverage sales; NPS improvement of 10 points.

---

### Experiment 3: Memory Vessel

**Hypothesis:** Serving NA beverages in distinctive, branded glassware with an accompanying origin story card will increase willingness to pay by 15% and improve brand recall at 30 days.

**Method:** Develop proprietary glassware and story cards for a premium NA spirit brand. A/B test in 8 venues: control group receives standard service; test group receives the "Memory Vessel" experience. Measure price sensitivity via Van Westendorp analysis, and conduct 30-day follow-up surveys on brand recall and purchase intent.

**Success metric:** 15% increase in willingness to pay; 40% unaided brand recall at 30 days (vs. 20% baseline).
