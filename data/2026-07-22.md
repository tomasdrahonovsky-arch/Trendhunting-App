## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a tension between heritage and reinvention, with established institutions grappling to remain relevant while new generations redefine what authenticity, community, and value mean to them. The fine wine industry's existential question—whether Spain's top sparkling wines should abandon the "Cava" name to escape category stigma—mirrors broader anxieties about legacy brands trapped by their own history. Meanwhile, younger collectors are proving appetite exists for premium experiences, but only when accessed through transparent, community-driven channels rather than opaque gatekeeping systems.

The hospitality sector is bifurcating sharply: Marston's aggressive pivot toward sports-led "Grandstand" pubs (30% like-for-like growth) demonstrates that communal viewing experiences remain potent social glue, while independent breweries like Three Acre pursue intimate taproom models rooted in hyperlocal identity. Both succeed by offering belonging—just at different scales.

Geopolitical volatility continues reshaping beverage flows. Trump's 50% tariffs on Canadian imports over provincial alcohol bans signal that drinks have become diplomatic weapons, with trade routes increasingly subject to political whim rather than market logic. Brands must build supply chain resilience and geographic diversification into their core strategy.

The wellness-adjacent space sees regenerative agriculture moving from marketing claim to operational imperative, with LVMH's Château Galoupet investing in hydrology systems and soil health as climate adaptation. This signals that sustainability credentials will increasingly require demonstrable, technical proof rather than aspirational language.

For non-alcoholic beverages, the opportunity lies in serving the emerging "functional collector"—consumers who approach wellness drinks with the same connoisseurship, community-seeking, and complexity-appreciation that younger wine collectors bring to fine wine. The chase matters as much as the consumption.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Name Trap—Legacy Categories as Growth Ceilings

**What happened:** Industry experts at London's Taste Spain event argued that Spain's finest sparkling wines should abandon the "Cava" designation entirely, with top producers like Recaredo and Gramona already defecting to the stricter "Corpinnat" collective mark.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a fundamental shift in how premium positioning works—established category names that once conferred legitimacy now impose ceilings. The value has migrated from institutional endorsement to producer-specific reputation and stricter quality standards.

**Societal layer:** Status / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands should resist the temptation to cluster under generic category descriptors ("mocktails," "NA spirits"). Building distinctive brand identity outside established category architecture may prove more valuable than category legitimacy. Consider whether your category association elevates or constrains perception.

**What to monitor:** Premium NA brands creating proprietary category language; consumer response to brands that explicitly reject category norms; emergence of collective marks or certification systems in functional beverages.

### SIGNAL 2: The Complexity Collector—Young Fine Wine Buyers Seek Chase, Not Opacity

**What happened:** Areni Global research confirms that under-40 fine wine collectors globally share identical collecting journeys—learning through peer networks rather than family, transitioning from buyer to collector between ages 26-40, and actively seeking complexity over opacity. Goedhuis Waddesdon reports success targeting under-35s who "want to taste good stuff, learn about good stuff, interact with it."

**Why it is a signal:** Younger affluent consumers aren't rejecting premium categories—they're rejecting gatekeeping. They want the intellectual stimulation of complexity but demand transparent access channels. En primeur systems that once seemed obvious now "don't make sense" to this generation.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Control / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Premium NA beverages can capture this collector mindset by building complexity into products (botanical layering, terroir stories, vintage variations) while ensuring transparent, community-accessible purchasing. Create "the chase" through limited releases and allocation systems, but make the rules of engagement crystal clear.

**What to monitor:** Secondary market activity for limited NA releases; emergence of NA-focused collector communities on Discord/Substack; willingness to pay premiums for complexity and provenance in functional drinks.

### SIGNAL 3: Sports Venues as Social Infrastructure

**What happened:** Marston's Grandstand pubs—sports-led venues with stadium-like immersive environments—delivered 30% like-for-like sales growth, prompting the company to accelerate to 100 conversions in 2027. England World Cup match days drove 22% like-for-like growth, with Grandstand locations up 170% year-on-year.

**Why it is a signal:** In an era of atomized digital consumption, physical spaces purpose-built for collective emotional experiences are outperforming general hospitality. The investment in "tiered seating, advanced sound systems, and match-day promotions" signals that production value matters—passive viewing is insufficient.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Community / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Sports-viewing occasions represent massive NA opportunity, as extended sessions (90+ minutes) create natural moderation moments. Develop NA offerings specifically positioned for "match-day" occasions—sessionable, celebratory, shareable formats. Partner with sports-led venues on dedicated NA programs.

**What to monitor:** NA consumption patterns during major sporting events; venue-specific NA sales data during tournaments; emergence of sports-focused NA brands or product lines.

### SIGNAL 4: Hyperlocal Hospitality as Identity Anchor

**What happened:** Three Acre Brewery is converting a historic railway signal box and a High Street location into taprooms, receiving "significant backing from local residents" with crowdfunders assured their tokens will work across venues. The brewery emphasizes "roots in Heathfield since the very beginning."

**Why it is a signal:** Against consolidation and chain homogenization, consumers are investing emotional and financial capital in venues that anchor local identity. The signal box conversion—a repurposed piece of community infrastructure—represents hospitality as heritage preservation.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands can build loyalty by embedding in specific communities rather than pursuing national scale. Consider hyperlocal partnerships, venue-specific products, and community ownership models. The "local" credential increasingly requires proof of genuine rootedness, not just geographic labeling.

**What to monitor:** Crowdfunding success rates for independent beverage venues; consumer willingness to pay premiums for demonstrably local products; emergence of community-owned NA brands or taprooms.

### SIGNAL 5: Regenerative as Operational Reality, Not Marketing Claim

**What happened:** LVMH's Château Galoupet achieved regenerative organic certification in 2025 and is investing in hydrology systems—trenches along contour lines, restored historic reservoirs—to capture rainfall and enable emergency irrigation as climate adaptation infrastructure.

**Why it is a signal:** Sustainability claims are transitioning from brand positioning to operational necessity. The specificity of Galoupet's interventions (cover crops by macro-terroir, predator chain monitoring, water capture engineering) signals that vague environmental language will increasingly face scrutiny.

**Societal layer:** Control / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands built on wellness and purity positioning must prepare for elevated proof requirements. Invest in traceable, specific sustainability practices that can withstand interrogation. "Regenerative" will require technical demonstration, not just certification logos.

**What to monitor:** Consumer and media scrutiny of sustainability claims in beverages; emergence of third-party verification systems for regenerative practices; premium pricing power for demonstrably sustainable NA products.

### SIGNAL 6: Beverages as Geopolitical Weapons

**What happened:** Trump imposed 50% tariffs on Canadian imports after provinces removed US alcoholic beverages from government-controlled liquor stores, citing an 81% drop in Canadian imports of US alcohol. The White House explicitly framed alcohol restrictions as discrimination warranting trade retaliation.

**Why it is a signal:** Beverages have become explicit instruments of diplomatic conflict, with trade routes subject to political volatility rather than market dynamics. Provincial alcohol monopolies—once seen as regulatory quirks—are now geopolitical flashpoints.

**Societal layer:** Control / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Supply chain diversification is no longer optional. NA brands relying on single-source ingredients or concentrated production geographies face elevated risk. Build redundancy into sourcing; monitor political relationships between production and consumption markets.

**What to monitor:** Trade policy developments affecting beverage ingredients and finished goods; provincial/state-level alcohol policy changes; emergence of "origin nationalism" in beverage marketing.

Enable and amplify specific, personality-driven recommendations. Build discovery mechanisms that feel like peer recommendations, not algorithmic suggestions.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Complexity Drop—Limited NA Release with Collector Mechanics

**Hypothesis:** Younger consumers who exhibit "collector" behavior in fine wine will transfer that engagement to NA beverages if offered comparable complexity, scarcity, and transparent access mechanics.

**Method:** Develop a limited-edition NA botanical blend with documented terroir provenance and vintage dating. Release through an allocation system with clear, published rules (waitlist priority, purchase history, community participation). Create accompanying educational content explaining botanical sourcing, blending decisions, and flavor development. Price at premium tier (£25-40/bottle). Limit initial release to 500 bottles with announced future releases.

**Success metric:** Waitlist conversion rate >60%; secondary market activity (resale listings) within 30 days; repeat purchase intent >70% among initial buyers; social sharing of "unboxing" or tasting content.

---

### Experiment 2: Match-Day NA Program with Sports-Led Venue Partner

**Hypothesis:** Purpose-built sports-viewing venues represent underexploited NA occasions, with extended session lengths creating natural demand for sessionable, celebratory non-alcoholic options.

**Method:** Partner with a Grandstand-format or equivalent sports-led pub group to develop a dedicated "Match-Day NA" program. Create a 3-SKU range: pre-match energizing option, 90-minute sessionable core product, and post-match celebratory format. Design packaging and glassware that signals participation in collective experience (not abstention). Train staff on enthusiastic NA service. Measure against control venues without program.

**Success metric:** NA sales as percentage of total beverage sales >15% during match days (vs. industry average ~3-5%); customer satisfaction scores for NA drinkers equivalent to alcohol consumers; repeat visitation among NA-primary customers.

---

### Experiment 3: Hyperlocal NA Taproom Residency

**Hypothesis:** NA brands can build disproportionate loyalty by embedding in specific communities through venue partnerships that demonstrate genuine local commitment rather than pursuing national distribution.

**Method:** Partner with an independent brewery taproom (like Three Acre) to create a 6-month NA residency program. Develop a venue-exclusive NA product incorporating local ingredients with documented provenance. Host monthly community events (tastings, pairings, producer visits). Offer crowdfunder-style community investment in future product development. Measure brand awareness, purchase intent, and advocacy within 10-mile radius vs. control market.

**Success metric:** Unaided brand awareness within local market >40% (vs. <5% in control); Net Promoter Score >60 among taproom visitors; successful crowdfunding of follow-on product (>£10k raised); venue request for permanent listing post-residency.
