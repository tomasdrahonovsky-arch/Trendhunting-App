## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a fundamental tension: consumers are simultaneously demanding functional performance from their beverages while seeking deeper emotional and sensory experiences that transcend mere utility. The most significant signal is the mainstreaming of functional ingredients into traditional alcohol categories—Joel Gott's electrolyte-infused wine spritz represents not just product innovation but a strategic recruitment tool aimed at younger drinkers who have been conditioned by the $43 billion hydration drinks market to expect wellness benefits from everything they consume.

This functional creep is occurring alongside a counter-movement toward terroir, craft, and place-based authenticity. Prosecco producer Andreola is deliberately removing the word "Prosecco" from labels to emphasize specific vineyard identity over category recognition—a remarkable act of commercial self-sabotage that signals how premiumization now requires differentiation from mass-market associations. Climate adaptation is accelerating this shift, with Château d'Esclans trialing Greek, Spanish, and Italian grape varieties in Provence as harvest dates move ever earlier.

The wellness conversation is maturing beyond simple "better-for-you" claims. Weight loss drug side effects are under scrutiny, migraines are being reframed as multi-system conditions rather than "just headaches," and strength training is being interrogated for potentially perpetuating diet culture under a different guise. Consumers are becoming more sophisticated about health claims and more skeptical of quick fixes.

For non-alcoholic beverage brands, the opportunity lies in bridging functional credibility with emotional resonance. The brands that will win are those that deliver measurable benefits while creating cultural moments—whether through music collaborations, place-based storytelling, or community-building rituals that make drinking feel like belonging rather than mere consumption.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: Functional Ingredients Colonize Wine

**What happened:** Joel Gott Wines released Sauvy B, a canned Sauvignon Blanc spritz with electrolytes from pink Himalayan salt, positioned explicitly as a "recruitment tool" for younger drinkers rather than a product for existing wine consumers.

**Why it is a signal:** The $43 billion electrolyte drinks market is reshaping consumer expectations across all beverage categories. Wine—traditionally the most resistant category to functional innovation—is now adopting sports drink ingredients to remain relevant.

**Societal layer:** Control / wellness

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should view functional ingredients not as differentiators but as table stakes. The competitive advantage shifts to *which* functional benefits and *how* they're communicated. Electrolytes are now commoditized; the next frontier is adaptogens, nootropics, and mood-specific formulations.

**What to monitor:** Trinchero's sales data on Sauvy B; functional ingredient adoption rates among major wine and spirits conglomerates; consumer research on "functional expectations" by beverage category.

### SIGNAL 2: The Anti-Category Movement in Premium Beverages

**What happened:** Andreola, a benchmark Prosecco producer, has spent years deliberately removing the word "Prosecco" from labels to emphasize specific vineyard identity (Rive designations) over category recognition.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a rejection of category-level marketing in favor of terroir-based differentiation—a strategy that trades volume for margin and mass appeal for connoisseurship.

**Societal layer:** Status / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Premium NA brands should consider whether category association ("non-alcoholic beer," "mocktail") is limiting their positioning. Place-based, ingredient-origin, or process-based naming conventions may unlock higher price points and more loyal consumers.

**What to monitor:** Consumer willingness to pay premiums for origin-specific NA products; emergence of "single-origin" or "estate" positioning in NA categories.

### SIGNAL 3: Climate Forcing Ingredient Innovation

**What happened:** Château d'Esclans is trialing Greek, Spanish, and Italian grape varieties in Provence as harvest dates move to early August—the earliest in the estate's history.

**Why it is a signal:** Climate adaptation is no longer theoretical; it's forcing real-time ingredient substitution in traditional categories. This creates both supply chain disruption and opportunities for novel flavor profiles.

**Societal layer:** Control / survival

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should audit their botanical and ingredient supply chains for climate vulnerability. Simultaneously, "climate-adapted" or "future-proof" ingredients could become a marketing narrative—positioning brands as forward-thinking and sustainable.

**What to monitor:** Agricultural reports on heat-tolerant botanicals; consumer response to "climate-adapted" product positioning; insurance and supply chain data on ingredient availability.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: Climate-Adapted Botanical Line

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will pay a 20% premium for NA beverages positioned around "climate-resilient" or "future-proof" botanicals, particularly when the narrative includes specific origin stories and adaptation journeys.

**Method:** Develop a limited-edition NA botanical drink featuring heat-tolerant ingredients (e.g., Mediterranean herbs, drought-resistant citrus varieties) with transparent sourcing documentation. Test in three markets with different climate awareness levels. A/B test packaging that emphasizes "climate-adapted" messaging vs. standard premium positioning.

**Success metric:** 15% higher conversion rate on climate-adapted messaging; willingness-to-pay premium of ≥18% in consumer research; social media engagement rate 25% above brand baseline.
