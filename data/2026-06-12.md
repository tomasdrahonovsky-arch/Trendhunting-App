## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between authenticity and performance, heritage and reinvention. The drinks industry is caught in a moment where "selling joy" has become the strategic imperative—not shifting product, but connecting with how people feel. Mary Portas's keynote to the WSTA crystallises what's been building: consumers don't want palaces of stuff, they want sensory experiences, stories, and moments of meaning.

Three currents dominate. First, the rehabilitation of pleasure as a legitimate pursuit. From rosé diversity being celebrated rather than homogenised, to the historical debunking of pirate rum myths (they were actually moderate drinkers who invented cocktails), there's a cultural recalibration around how we frame indulgence. The "lipstick effect" framing—affordable pleasures surviving tough times—positions beverages as daily rituals rather than occasional treats.

Second, the emergence of experience-as-destination. Perelada positions itself as a "cultural destination where history, architecture, gastronomy, hospitality and wine converge." This isn't winery marketing; it's a template for how beverage brands must think about physical presence. The 127 hectares of organic vineyards become secondary to the story they enable.

Third, young people are simultaneously keeping cinema alive (78% of 16-34s are regular attendees) while losing faith in their futures. This paradox—seeking communal experiences while doubting individual outcomes—creates specific opportunities for brands that can offer belonging without demanding belief in meritocracy.

The strategic question for non-alcoholic beverages: how do you become the ritual that marks the transition between day parts, the social lubricant that doesn't require alcohol, the affordable pleasure that feels neither virtuous nor indulgent but simply good?

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Joy Positioning

**What happened:** Mary Portas told the UK wine and spirits trade to stop asking "how do I shift product?" and start asking "where am I selling joy?" She cited Blue Zones research—communities living to 100 drink wine daily—as a weapon against health messaging.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a strategic pivot from defensive positioning (responding to health headlines) to offensive framing (owning pleasure). The industry is being told to stop apologising and start claiming emotional territory.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands have been over-indexing on health benefits and under-indexing on joy. The opportunity is to position NA drinks as the "PM equivalent of a great morning coffee"—a ritual that marks transition, not a compromise that signals restriction.

**What to monitor:** How premium NA brands frame their marketing; whether "functional" messaging gives way to "pleasure" messaging; consumer response to joy-forward versus health-forward positioning.

---

### SIGNAL 2: Diversity as Differentiator

**What happened:** Patrick Schmitt MW's rosé analysis explicitly celebrates category diversity: "Wouldn't it be monotonous if every bottle tasted like a pale Provençal example?" Deep-coloured Spanish rosados are positioned as equally valid to pale French styles.

**Why it is a signal:** This challenges the homogenisation that often follows category growth. Rather than converging on a single "correct" expression, the market is being encouraged to fragment into distinct, defensible positions.

**Societal layer:** Identity / rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The NA category risks converging on a single aesthetic (clean, minimal, wellness-coded). Brands that embrace bold, unexpected expressions—the equivalent of "deep-coloured, generously fruited Spanish rosados"—may find underserved audiences.

**What to monitor:** Consumer fatigue with minimalist NA branding; emergence of maximalist or heritage-coded NA products; regional variations in NA preferences.

---

### SIGNAL 3: Heritage as Living Practice

**What happened:** The oldest gin/genever brands list reveals continuous production spanning 350+ years. Simultaneously, China's wine industry is positioning its 6th-century BC winemaking tradition as foundation for premium positioning.

**Why it is a signal:** Heritage is being reframed from dusty credential to living practice. The emphasis is on continuous adaptation within tradition, not museum-piece preservation.

**Societal layer:** Identity / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands lack heritage by definition—the category is young. The opportunity is to borrow heritage from adjacent traditions (botanical knowledge, fermentation practices, regional ingredients) or to explicitly position as "new tradition in formation."

**What to monitor:** How established spirits brands extend heritage positioning to NA line extensions; whether NA-native brands attempt heritage construction; consumer response to "new tradition" framing.

---

### SIGNAL 4: Cinema as Community Infrastructure

**What happened:** Research shows 78% of 16-34s attend cinema regularly, with young people explicitly citing community and "wellness" benefits. This contradicts narratives of streaming-induced isolation.

**Why it is a signal:** Young people are actively seeking shared physical experiences despite digital alternatives. The cinema isn't competing with Netflix; it's competing with loneliness.

**Societal layer:** Community / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues that facilitate shared experience—not just consumption—will capture this cohort. NA beverages positioned as "what you drink at the cinema" or "what you share before the film" could claim ritual territory currently owned by popcorn and soda.

**What to monitor:** Cinema concession innovation; emergence of "third place" venues combining film/content with beverage service; young consumer language around why they attend physical venues.

---

### SIGNAL 5: Youth Pessimism Meets Selective Spending

**What happened:** IPPR reports 16-21 year-olds are "losing faith in their futures" with record fears of long-term unemployment. Simultaneously, this cohort maintains specific spending on experiences they value.

**Why it is a signal:** Economic pessimism doesn't produce uniform austerity—it produces ruthless prioritisation. Young people will spend on what delivers meaning while cutting everything else.

**Societal layer:** Control / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Price sensitivity will coexist with willingness to pay premium for the right experience. NA brands must earn their place in the "worth it" category through meaning, not just quality. The question isn't "can they afford it?" but "does it matter enough?"

**What to monitor:** Youth spending patterns on experiences versus goods; which beverage occasions survive budget cuts; language young consumers use to justify discretionary spending.

---

### SIGNAL 6: The Myth-Correction Impulse

**What happened:** Deep-dive content debunking pirate rum mythology reveals pirates were actually moderate drinkers who diluted rum with water and invented early cocktails. The "manic-eyed madmen" image is explicitly called "reductive."

**Why it is a signal:** There's appetite for nuanced historical truth over simplified mythology. Consumers want to feel smarter than the cliché, not complicit in it.

**Societal layer:** Identity / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brand storytelling that acknowledges and subverts expectations ("you think you know, but actually...") may resonate more than straightforward heritage claims. The NA category can position moderation as historically sophisticated rather than modern restriction.

**What to monitor:** Consumer response to "myth-busting" content; whether brands successfully reframe moderation as historical norm; appetite for complexity in brand narratives.

---

### SIGNAL 7: AI Spirituality's Uncanny Valley

**What happened:** Dazed investigates AI-generated spiritual content, noting a monk video that felt "off" despite offering reasonable advice. The question posed: can digital gurus answer burning spiritual questions?

**Why it is a signal:** Spiritual content is being automated, but consumers are detecting the absence of authentic presence. This creates both threat (commoditised wellness content) and opportunity (premium on genuine human connection).

**Societal layer:** Spirituality / authenticity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands using AI-generated content for wellness/mindfulness positioning risk the "uncanny valley" effect. Human-made, imperfect, clearly authored content may command premium attention. Hospitality experiences with genuine human presence become more valuable as digital alternatives proliferate.

**What to monitor:** Consumer ability to detect AI-generated wellness content; premium placed on "human-made" credentials; backlash against automated spiritual/wellness messaging.

---

### SIGNAL 8: Destination Convergence

**What happened:** Perelada winery explicitly positions as "cultural destination where history, architecture, gastronomy, hospitality and wine converge"—not a winery that also does tourism, but a destination that happens to make wine.

**Why it is a signal:** The hierarchy is inverting. Product becomes enabler of experience rather than experience being marketing for product.

**Societal layer:** Status / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands with physical presence should consider whether the product serves the place or the place serves the product. Taprooms, tasting rooms, and brand homes may need to lead with experience and let product follow.

**What to monitor:** Investment in brand physical spaces; consumer language about why they visit beverage destinations; which elements of destination experience drive purchase intent.

---

### SIGNAL 9: The Body Count Deflation

**What happened:** Survey reveals Americans report 20 lifetime sexual partners on average but tell partners only 9. Nearly 30% intentionally lower their number, subtracting an average of 26 partners.

**Why it is a signal:** Despite "sex-positive" cultural messaging, people still perform moderation when stakes are personal. Public values and private behaviour remain misaligned.

**Societal layer:** Identity / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Consumers may publicly embrace "drink less, drink better" messaging while privately maintaining different behaviours. Brands should design for private consumption contexts, not just public performance of moderation.

**What to monitor:** Gap between stated drinking intentions and actual behaviour; private versus social consumption patterns for NA beverages; whether NA consumption is performed or genuine.

---

### SIGNAL 10: Formula 1 as Cultural Gateway

**What happened:** Jack Daniel's Halo MK1 partnership with McLaren explicitly targets new demographics reached through F1's popularity explosion, driven by Liberty Media ownership and social media coverage.

**Why it is a signal:** Sports partnerships are being reframed as cultural access points rather than logo placement. The question isn't "who watches F1?" but "who does F1 connect us to?"

**Societal layer:** Status / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands seeking younger demographics should evaluate cultural properties not by audience size but by cultural currency. F1, gaming, and emerging sports offer access to audiences that traditional beverage marketing cannot reach.

**What to monitor:** Which cultural properties NA brands pursue; consumer response to NA/sports partnerships; whether F1's audience growth translates to beverage category growth.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Joy Audit

**Hypothesis:** NA beverage marketing over-indexes on health/wellness messaging and under-indexes on pleasure/joy messaging, creating positioning vulnerability as competitors claim emotional territory.

**Method:** Conduct content analysis of top 20 NA brands' marketing materials, scoring ratio of health-forward versus joy-forward messaging. Survey 500 NA consumers on purchase drivers, comparing stated importance of health benefits versus pleasure/enjoyment. Test two versions of identical product with different positioning (wellness-coded versus joy-coded) in blind purchase intent study.

**Success metric:** Identify optimal health/joy messaging ratio that maximises purchase intent; quantify the "joy gap" in current NA marketing; establish baseline for repositioning strategy.

---

### Experiment 2: The Cinema Ritual

**Hypothesis:** Young consumers seeking communal experiences will adopt NA beverages as cinema ritual if positioned as "what you drink at the movies" rather than "healthy alternative to soda."

**Method:** Partner with independent cinema chain to offer curated NA beverage pairing with specific film genres (botanical spritz with arthouse, ginger-forward drinks with action, etc.). Track purchase rates, repeat behaviour, and social sharing. Survey participants on whether beverage enhanced experience and likelihood to repeat.

**Success metric:** 15% attachment rate (beverage purchased with ticket); 40% of purchasers report beverage "enhanced" experience; measurable social sharing of beverage/film pairing.

---

### Experiment 3: The Moderation Heritage Play

**Hypothesis:** Positioning moderation as historically sophisticated (pirates diluted rum, Blue Zone centenarians drink moderately) will resonate more than positioning moderation as modern health choice.

**Method:** Develop two brand narrative versions for identical NA product: one emphasising modern wellness benefits, one emphasising historical precedent for moderate/mindful drinking. Test both narratives with 1,000 consumers aged 25-45, measuring brand perception, purchase intent, and willingness to pay premium. Track which narrative generates more organic sharing and conversation.

**Success metric:** Heritage-framed narrative achieves equal or higher purchase intent than wellness-framed narrative; heritage narrative generates 20%+ more organic sharing; heritage narrative supports higher price point tolerance.
