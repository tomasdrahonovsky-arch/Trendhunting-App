## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between authenticity and accessibility, as consumers increasingly demand products and experiences that honour craft, provenance, and complexity while simultaneously fitting into more mindful, moderated lifestyles. The non-alcoholic rosé surge (Article 5) crystallises this moment perfectly: consumers want the aspirational lifestyle signifiers of premium wine culture without the alcohol, and crucially, they're no longer willing to accept inferior quality as the trade-off.

Three interconnected forces are shaping beverage culture. First, the "terroir-ification" of spirits continues its march, with mezcal positioning itself as wine's spiritual cousin (Article 1) and Korean wheat soju being revived as a heritage craft category (Article 11). This signals that consumers are developing increasingly sophisticated palates for provenance narratives across all drink categories—a framework non-alcoholic brands must adopt. Second, the "zebra striping" phenomenon—alternating between alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks within a single occasion—has moved from trend to established behaviour, fundamentally reshaping how hospitality venues must think about their offerings.

Third, and perhaps most significant for brand builders, is the emergence of what we might call "trust-based consumption." From the Austrian winemaker who works "with trust rather than control" (Article 2) to the climbing gym cafés that "turn it over to the public" (Article 18), successful operators are ceding control to their communities rather than dictating experiences. This represents a fundamental shift in the brand-consumer relationship.

The health consciousness thread running through multiple articles—from calorie-aware spritz consumption (Article 7) to AI-assisted health decisions (Article 25)—suggests that wellness remains a powerful purchase driver, but one that consumers increasingly want to navigate on their own terms, with transparency rather than prescription.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Terroir-ification of Everything

**What happened:** Mezcal is being explicitly positioned as "the spirit for wine lovers" through terroir narratives, with producers emphasising soil, altitude, microclimate, and varietal expression as primary value drivers (Article 1).

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the maturation of craft spirits marketing beyond "artisanal" into genuinely wine-like complexity narratives. When a spirit category can credibly claim that "two Espadín agaves can taste completely different depending on which side of the hill they're grown," it establishes a new floor for how premium beverages must communicate their value.

**Societal layer:** Status / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands must develop equally sophisticated provenance stories. This means moving beyond "dealcoholised wine" positioning toward origin-specific narratives: the specific tea gardens, botanical sources, or water sources that create distinctive flavour profiles. Hospitality venues should consider "terroir flights" of non-alcoholic options.

**What to monitor:** Premium non-alc brands investing in single-origin storytelling; sommelier adoption of terroir language for non-alcoholic pairings; consumer willingness to pay premiums for provenance-verified NA beverages.

---

### SIGNAL 2: Zebra Striping Goes Mainstream

**What happened:** The no/low rosé category is being driven by "zebra striping"—consumers alternating between alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks within a single occasion. Over 90% of non-alc consumers also buy alcohol (Article 5).

**Why it is a signal:** This definitively ends the false binary between "drinkers" and "non-drinkers." The market is not about conversion but about occasion expansion. Moderation is now a behaviour, not an identity.

**Societal layer:** Control / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues must design for seamless switching, not separate "mocktail menus" that signal otherness. This means pricing parity, glassware parity, and menu integration. Brands should consider "session" positioning—products designed to be consumed alongside their alcoholic counterparts.

**What to monitor:** Menu design innovations that integrate NA options; pricing strategies that don't penalise moderation; social media discourse around "switching" occasions.

---

### SIGNAL 3: The Calorie Consciousness Paradox

**What happened:** Spritzes are being promoted as lower-calorie alternatives to other cocktails—and even to "healthy" drinks like matcha lattes and smoothies (Article 7). However, the article carefully notes these are "empty calories" versus nutritionally beneficial ones.

**Why it is a signal:** Consumers are performing increasingly sophisticated caloric calculus, but the framing reveals a tension: they want permission to indulge while maintaining health consciousness. The comparison to matcha is particularly telling—it positions alcoholic drinks in direct competition with wellness beverages.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic beverages have a significant opportunity to win the "functional indulgence" space—delivering both the ritual pleasure of a cocktail AND nutritional benefit. Brands should consider transparent calorie labelling as a competitive advantage, not a liability.

**What to monitor:** Consumer discourse around "worth it" calories; functional ingredient integration in NA cocktails; wellness influencer adoption of specific NA brands.

### SIGNAL 4: Heritage Revival as Authenticity Play

**What happened:** A Korean entrepreneur is reviving 480-year-old wheat-based soju traditions (Article 11), while a Polish distillery with 180 years of history is being auctioned as a "turnkey" heritage brand opportunity (Article 6).

**Why it is a signal:** In an era of synthetic authenticity, genuinely historical production methods and recipes are becoming scarce, valuable assets. The Korean soju revival is particularly notable—it's positioned against the "green bottled" mass-market soju as a craft alternative.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Historical beverage traditions that predate or exist outside alcohol culture represent untapped positioning opportunities. Consider: traditional tea ceremonies, historical botanical preparations, or regional non-alcoholic fermentation traditions. Authenticity cannot be manufactured, but it can be rediscovered.

**What to monitor:** Academic and culinary interest in pre-industrial beverage traditions; consumer response to "revival" narratives; investment in heritage beverage brands.

### SIGNAL 5: Trust-Based Production Philosophy

**What happened:** An Austrian winemaker describes working "with trust rather than control," emphasising restraint over intervention (Article 2). Separately, a climbing gym café operator describes needing to "turn it over to the public" to succeed (Article 18).

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a philosophical shift from brand-as-authority to brand-as-steward. In both production and hospitality, the most resonant positioning is now about what you *don't* do—minimal intervention, letting ingredients or communities express themselves.

**Societal layer:** Control / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands should consider "restraint narratives"—what they've chosen NOT to add, manipulate, or control. This applies to both product formulation (minimal processing, no artificial ingredients) and brand behaviour (community-led product development, transparent sourcing).

**What to monitor:** Consumer response to "minimal intervention" claims; success of community-driven product development; backlash against over-engineered functional beverages.

### SIGNAL 6: Third Spaces Require Hybrid Flexibility

**What happened:** A café operator in a climbing gym describes how the space unexpectedly became a popular remote working destination, requiring ongoing adaptation to serve both climbers and laptop workers (Article 18).

**Why it is a signal:** The "third space" is no longer a fixed concept but a negotiated one. Successful venues must accommodate multiple use cases simultaneously, and the community ultimately determines the space's function.

**Societal layer:** Community / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Beverage programs must support multiple occasion types within the same venue and timeframe. This means all-day menus that transition seamlessly, products that work for both focused work and social gathering, and pricing structures that don't penalise lingering.

**What to monitor:** Hybrid venue concepts; beverage consumption patterns across different use occasions in the same space; community feedback mechanisms in hospitality.

### SIGNAL 7: The Phone Call Is Dead (And What Replaces It)

**What happened:** 69% of Gen Z keep phones on silent; 74% ignore unfamiliar calls; 64% prefer texts to calls (Article 39). This is framed not as rudeness but as rational adaptation to a spam-polluted communication channel.

**Why it is a signal:** The dominant communication mode for an entire generation has shifted from synchronous to asynchronous. This has profound implications for how brands reach consumers and how hospitality experiences are discovered and booked.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Phone-based reservation systems and customer service are increasingly friction points. Brands must meet consumers in asynchronous channels—text, DM, app-based communication. Discovery happens through visual social platforms, not phone calls or even websites.

**What to monitor:** Booking and reservation platform innovations; brand adoption of text-based customer service; Gen Z discovery patterns for new venues and products.

### SIGNAL 8: Economic Nationalism Reshapes Beverage Preferences

**What happened:** Canadian wine producers report 40-60% domestic sales increases since the US alcohol ban, describing it as "an ongoing small act of consumer resistance" (Article 14).

**Why it is a signal:** Geopolitical tensions are directly influencing beverage purchasing decisions. Consumers are using their purchasing power as a form of political expression, and this creates both opportunities and vulnerabilities for brands based on their national origin.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Origin stories must be carefully considered in the context of geopolitical sentiment. Local and regional sourcing becomes not just a sustainability play but a political one. Brands should consider how their supply chains and production locations might be perceived in different markets.

**What to monitor:** Consumer sentiment around country-of-origin; local beverage brand performance during trade tensions; social media discourse connecting purchasing to political expression.

### SIGNAL 9: AI as Health Intermediary

**What happened:** One in seven UK adults are using AI chatbots for health advice instead of seeing GPs, with one in four citing long NHS waiting lists as the reason (Article 25).

**Why it is a signal:** Consumers are increasingly comfortable with AI as a trusted advisor for personal decisions, including health. This normalises AI-mediated decision-making across other domains, including food and beverage choices.

**Societal layer:** Control / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** AI-powered beverage recommendation and personalisation will become expected, not novel. Brands should consider how their products might be recommended by AI health advisors—what claims, ingredients, and benefits would trigger positive recommendations?

**What to monitor:** AI chatbot recommendations for beverage categories; integration of beverage data into health apps; consumer trust in AI-mediated product recommendations.

### SIGNAL 10: Sleep Compatibility as Relationship Infrastructure

**What happened:** Research indicates one in three couples are "sleep incompatible," with different chronotypes causing relationship friction. 74% of couples regularly go to bed at different times (Article 37).

**Why it is a signal:** The granular mechanics of daily life—including when and how people wind down—are being recognised as relationship infrastructure. This creates new occasion spaces around individual evening routines rather than shared couple rituals.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Evening beverage occasions may be more individual than assumed. Products positioned for personal wind-down rituals (rather than shared social occasions) may find receptive audiences. Consider: single-serve formats, personalised functional benefits, and positioning around individual sleep/relaxation needs.

**What to monitor:** Growth in single-serve evening beverage formats; discourse around individual vs. shared evening rituals; sleep and relaxation functional beverage innovation.

---

### SIGNAL 11: The Puffer-Fish Pattern in Consumer Relationships

**What happened:** Dating experts describe "puffer-fishing"—pushing away when intimacy increases—as a common pattern rooted in avoidant attachment styles (Article 38).

**Why it is a signal:** While framed as dating advice, this pattern applies to brand relationships. Consumers who engage intensely with brands may exhibit similar push-pull behaviour, especially when brands attempt to deepen the relationship through loyalty programs or personalisation.

**Societal layer:** Control / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brand relationship building should respect consumer ambivalence. Overly aggressive loyalty programs or personalisation may trigger "puffer-fish" responses. Consider: opt-in depth rather than assumed intimacy; allowing consumers to control relationship intensity.

**What to monitor:** Loyalty program opt-out rates; consumer backlash to personalisation; success of "low-pressure" brand relationship models.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Terroir Flight Programme

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will pay premium prices for non-alcoholic beverages when presented with wine-style terroir narratives and comparative tasting experiences.

**Method:** Partner with 3-5 upscale restaurants to offer "NA Terroir Flights"—curated selections of non-alcoholic beverages (kombuchas, shrubs, botanical waters, dealcoholised wines) presented with origin cards detailing specific growing regions, production methods, and flavour profiles. Train staff to present these with sommelier-level knowledge. Price at parity with wine flights. Run for 8 weeks, comparing to control venues offering standard NA menus.

**Success metric:** Average NA beverage spend per cover increases by 40% vs. control; 25% of tables ordering NA flights report they would order again; staff report increased confidence discussing NA options.

---

### Experiment 2: The Zebra Stripe Menu Integration

**Hypothesis:** Integrating non-alcoholic options directly into cocktail menu categories (rather than separate "mocktail" sections) will increase NA beverage orders and reduce social friction around ordering.

**Method:** Redesign cocktail menus at 5 partner bars to present each cocktail category (e.g., "Refreshing," "Spirit-Forward," "Celebratory") with both alcoholic and non-alcoholic options side by side, with identical pricing and glassware specifications. Track ordering patterns, customer feedback, and staff observations over 12 weeks. Compare to 5 control venues with traditional separated menus.

**Success metric:** NA beverage orders increase by 50% vs. control; customer surveys indicate reduced "awkwardness" when ordering NA options; no decrease in overall beverage revenue.

---

### Experiment 3: The Trust Transparency Label

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will demonstrate preference for non-alcoholic beverages that communicate "restraint" and minimal intervention through transparent labelling.

**Method:** Develop a "Trust Label" format for NA beverages that prominently displays: (1) what's NOT in the product (no artificial sweeteners, no added sugars, etc.), (2) number of ingredients, (3) processing method simplicity score. Test across three product lines in retail settings using A/B shelf placement. Conduct exit interviews with purchasers about decision factors.

**Success metric:** Products with Trust Labels show 30% higher velocity than control packaging; exit interviews confirm label influenced purchase decision in >40% of cases; social media sharing of label format indicates organic adoption.
