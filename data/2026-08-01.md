## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a fundamental tension between heritage and reinvention—a moment where authenticity is being actively reconstructed rather than merely preserved. The drinks industry is witnessing simultaneous movements: established players scrambling to defend territory (US wine's collective marketing crisis, Endeavour Group offloading winery assets) while insurgent categories gain ground through cultural resonance rather than advertising spend (Hugo Spritz overtaking Aperol in UK searches, regenerative agriculture entering beer through Wildfarmed).

The most significant undercurrent is the emergence of "purpose as product"—consumers increasingly expect beverages to embody values rather than merely communicate them. Groove Armada's Do It Right lager, brewed with regeneratively farmed barley, exemplifies this: the farming practice isn't a marketing story bolted onto a beer; it's the reason the beer exists. This represents a maturation beyond sustainability claims toward integrated value propositions.

Meanwhile, the hospitality sector is fragmenting into hyper-local experiences. Federico Penzo's "Back to Roots" programme at Bodrum Edition, Coworth Park's English wine trolley service, and Montreal's celebrated culinary diversity all point toward place-based authenticity as the new luxury differentiator. Generic "premium" positioning is losing ground to specific provenance stories.

The health and wellness conversation continues its quiet expansion, with social prescribing (hobbies as medicine) gaining institutional legitimacy and supplement scepticism rising. For non-alcoholic beverages, this creates both opportunity and scrutiny—functional claims will face increasing consumer sophistication.

The strategic imperative: brands must choose between defending legacy positions through collective action or building new relevance through cultural integration. The middle ground—incremental innovation with conventional marketing—appears increasingly untenable.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Spritz Succession

**What happened:** Hugo Spritz has overtaken Aperol Spritz in UK summer Google searches for the first time, with indexed interest averaging 3.6% higher across the 13-week period ending July 12, 2026. In 2022, Hugo generated just 2.3% of Aperol's search interest.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the maturation of the spritz category beyond a single brand into a format that consumers actively explore and customise. The shift toward elderflower (lighter, more floral) over bitter orange suggests evolving palate preferences toward subtlety and sessionability.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The spritz format—effervescent, low-ABV, visually distinctive—translates directly to non-alcoholic applications. St-Germain's decision to focus on "growing the core brand" rather than RTDs suggests a gap for NA spritz formats that capture the ritual without the alcohol. Hospitality venues should develop signature NA spritz programmes with equal presentation investment.

**What to monitor:** Search trends for "non-alcoholic spritz" and "zero-proof aperitivo"; emergence of dedicated NA bitter/botanical brands; spritz menu penetration in casual dining.

### SIGNAL 2: Regenerative Agriculture Enters Beverage Identity

**What happened:** Signature Brew partnered with Groove Armada's Andy Cato and Wildfarmed to create Do It Right, a Czech-style lager brewed with regeneratively farmed malted barley. The collaboration explicitly positions the farming practice as central to the product's identity, not peripheral.

**Why it is a signal:** This moves beyond "sustainable sourcing" claims toward agriculture-as-brand-pillar. The involvement of a cultural figure (Cato) who literally sold his music rights to become a farmer adds biographical authenticity that marketing cannot fabricate.

**Societal layer:** Community / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Regenerative agriculture offers NA brands a differentiation axis that doesn't rely on mimicking alcohol or making functional claims. Ingredient provenance stories—specific farms, named growers, documented soil health improvements—could become the terroir equivalent for non-alcoholic beverages.

**What to monitor:** Wildfarmed's expansion into other beverage categories; consumer willingness to pay premiums for regenerative claims; emergence of regenerative certification standards for beverages.

### SIGNAL 3: The Collective Marketing Deficit

**What happened:** Gino Colangelo, founder of a major US wine PR agency, publicly criticised the American wine industry's failure to fund collective marketing, describing support for his pro-wine campaign as "frankly, at times a bit discouraging" and arriving in "fits and starts."

**Why it is a signal:** While dairy, almonds, and cranberries have established collective marketing bodies in the US, wine does not—leaving the category vulnerable to coordinated anti-alcohol messaging without coordinated defence. This structural weakness is being acknowledged by industry insiders.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The NA category has an opportunity to build collective marketing infrastructure before fragmentation sets in. Early coordination on category-level messaging (what NA beverages represent culturally, not just what they lack) could establish positioning that individual brands benefit from collectively.

**What to monitor:** Formation of NA beverage trade associations; category-level advertising spend versus brand-level; regulatory developments around health claims for NA products.

### SIGNAL 4: Social Prescribing Goes Mainstream

**What happened:** The Guardian profiled the growing practice of "social prescribing"—doctors referring patients to community-based activities like sea swimming rather than (or alongside) medication. The UK's Chill Therapy programme, an eight-week sea swimming course, was highlighted as a treatment for major depressive disorder.

**Why it is a signal:** Healthcare systems are institutionalising the therapeutic value of community activities and nature exposure. This legitimises "wellness" claims while simultaneously raising the bar for what counts as genuinely beneficial versus marketing-speak.

**Societal layer:** Mental health / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues and brands that facilitate genuine community formation—not just consumption occasions—gain alignment with healthcare trends. NA beverages positioned around specific activities (post-swim, post-hike, creative practice) could develop prescriber relationships or community partnerships that alcohol brands cannot access.

**What to monitor:** NHS social prescribing programme expansion; emergence of "link workers" as a channel; insurance coverage for activity-based interventions.

### SIGNAL 5: Hyper-Local Hospitality as Luxury Differentiator

**What happened:** Multiple high-end hospitality venues are centring hyper-local sourcing: Bodrum Edition's "Back to Roots" cocktail programme uses Bodrum mandarin fruit; Coworth Park's sommelier serves English wine from a branded trolley with estate honey and meadow hay in cocktails; Montreal's food scene is celebrated for its neighbourhood-specific culinary diversity.

**Why it is a signal:** "Local" has evolved from a sourcing claim to an experience design principle. The specificity matters—not "local honey" but "estate honey"; not "regional citrus" but "Bodrum mandarin fruit with oils extracted separately."

**Societal layer:** Status / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverage programmes in hospitality should develop location-specific signatures that cannot be replicated elsewhere. This requires investment in local botanical sourcing, named producer relationships, and staff training to articulate provenance stories with the same fluency as wine sommeliers.

**What to monitor:** Emergence of "NA sommeliers" or equivalent roles; local botanical cultivation programmes at hospitality venues; guest willingness to pay premiums for provenance-specific NA options.

### SIGNAL 6: Celebrity Co-Creation Deepens

**What happened:** Harrison Ford collaborated with Glenmorangie's Dr Bill Lumsden to develop a limited-edition whisky, exchanging samples across the Atlantic over several months rather than simply lending his name. The release is positioned around Ford's actual flavour preferences.

**Why it is a signal:** Celebrity beverage partnerships are evolving from endorsement to documented co-creation. The narrative emphasis on process (sample exchanges, preference articulation) suggests consumers now require evidence of genuine involvement.

**Societal layer:** Status / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands pursuing celebrity partnerships should structure them around verifiable co-creation rather than endorsement. The documentation of the creative process becomes content itself. This raises the authenticity bar but also creates differentiation from superficial partnerships.

**What to monitor:** Consumer scepticism metrics around celebrity beverage launches; social media engagement on "behind the scenes" co-creation content versus launch announcements; emergence of celebrity-founded NA brands versus celebrity-endorsed.

### SIGNAL 7: Heritage Revival as Market Entry Strategy

**What happened:** Lion Brewery Co, founded in London in 1836 and revived in Singapore in 2018, has returned to India after 60+ years, launching in Delhi's premium beer market. The brand explicitly connects its history to the origins of India Pale Ale.

**Why it is a signal:** Heritage brands are being revived and redeployed into growth markets, using historical narratives as differentiation in crowded premium segments. The strategy treats brand archaeology as a competitive asset.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Historical temperance beverages, botanical cordials, and traditional non-alcoholic preparations represent untapped heritage assets. Brands that can authentically revive and recontextualise these traditions gain narrative depth that new brands cannot manufacture.

**What to monitor:** Acquisition activity around dormant heritage beverage brands; consumer response to "revived" versus "new" brand positioning; success of Lion Brewery's India expansion as a template.

### SIGNAL 8: Asset Rationalisation in Traditional Wine

**What happened:** Endeavour Group is selling Chapel Hill Estate's physical assets (vineyards, cellar door) while retaining the brand, explicitly suggesting the property could become "a luxury tourism destination, hospitality venture, accommodation retreat" rather than continuing wine production.

**Why it is a signal:** Major drinks companies are separating brand value from production assets, suggesting that vineyard ownership is becoming a liability rather than an asset for some portfolios. The explicit pivot toward hospitality/tourism indicates where value is perceived to be migrating.

**Societal layer:** Status / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Wine tourism infrastructure may become available for repurposing. NA beverage brands or hospitality operators could acquire vineyard properties for experience-based offerings that don't require wine production—botanical gardens, wellness retreats, or NA beverage development facilities with agricultural credibility.

**What to monitor:** Vineyard property transaction volumes and buyer profiles; emergence of non-wine uses for traditional wine tourism infrastructure; Endeavour's success in maintaining brand value without production assets.

### SIGNAL 9: Earlier Socialising Among Young Adults

**What happened:** Data cited in the Hugo Spritz coverage indicates 37% of British 18-29 year-olds say they are socialising earlier than a year ago, rising to 42% among 30-44 year-olds. The spritz is now the number-one summer cocktail choice among British 18-29 year-olds.

**Why it is a signal:** Daylight socialising is becoming normalised among demographics previously associated with late-night drinking occasions. This represents a structural shift in when and how social drinking occurs, with implications for venue formats and beverage choices.

**Societal layer:** Community / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Earlier socialising occasions favour lower-ABV and non-alcoholic options—people who plan to be functional in the evening choose differently than those ending their night. NA beverages should be positioned for afternoon and early evening occasions, with hospitality venues developing dedicated daytime social programming.

**What to monitor:** Venue revenue distribution by daypart; emergence of dedicated afternoon social venues; NA beverage sales by time of day in on-trade.

---

### SIGNAL 10: Supplement Scepticism Rising

**What happened:** The Guardian published a dietitian-led analysis questioning the benefits of popular supplements including multivitamins, collagen, and magnesium, noting that "for most people, there isn't evidence of a huge benefit" from multivitamins.

**Why it is a signal:** Mainstream media is increasingly scrutinising functional health claims, creating a more sceptical consumer environment for products positioned around wellness benefits. The expert-led debunking format suggests growing appetite for evidence-based assessment.

**Societal layer:** Control / Mental health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional NA beverages making health claims face heightened scrutiny. Brands should either invest in robust clinical evidence or pivot positioning away from specific health claims toward experiential benefits (taste, ritual, social context) that don't require scientific validation.

**What to monitor:** Regulatory action on functional beverage claims; consumer research on claim scepticism by category; emergence of third-party verification standards for functional beverages.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Provenance Trolley

**Hypothesis:** Guests at premium hospitality venues will pay a 40%+ premium for non-alcoholic beverages when presented via tableside service with detailed provenance storytelling, mirroring the wine trolley model demonstrated at Coworth Park.

**Method:** Partner with three luxury hotels to develop dedicated NA beverage trolley programmes featuring five rotating options, each with documented single-source ingredients (named farm, specific harvest, grower biography). Train staff to deliver 90-second provenance narratives. Track order rates, price sensitivity, and guest feedback versus standard NA menu offerings over 12 weeks.

**Success metric:** NA beverage revenue per cover increases 25%+ versus control period; guest satisfaction scores for NA options reach parity with alcoholic beverage scores; repeat order rate exceeds 30%.

---

### Experiment 2: Regenerative Ingredient Collective

**Hypothesis:** A consortium of NA beverage brands sharing regenerative ingredient sourcing will achieve lower per-unit costs and stronger consumer resonance than individual brand sustainability initiatives.

**Method:** Recruit 5-7 non-competing NA beverage brands to form a purchasing collective for Wildfarmed-certified or equivalent regenerative ingredients. Develop shared certification mark and consumer education materials. Track ingredient costs, consumer awareness of regenerative claims, and purchase intent across participating brands versus non-participating competitors over 18 months.

**Success metric:** Ingredient costs reduced 15%+ versus individual sourcing; consumer recognition of collective mark reaches 20% among target demographic; participating brands show 10%+ sales lift versus category average.

---

### Experiment 3: The Afternoon Social Programme

**Hypothesis:** Dedicated 2-6pm programming featuring NA-forward beverage menus will generate profitable new revenue streams for venues currently underutilised during afternoon hours, capturing the "earlier socialising" trend.

**Method:** Partner with six urban venues (mix of bars, cafes, hotel lobbies) to develop "Afternoon Social" programming: curated NA spritz menus, ambient music, and social seating configurations. Market specifically to 25-40 demographic via workplace and residential channels. Track covers, average spend, NA-to-alcoholic ratio, and return visit rates over 16 weeks versus same-venue evening performance.

**Success metric:** Afternoon daypart contributes 20%+ of weekly revenue; NA beverages represent 50%+ of afternoon beverage sales; customer acquisition cost for afternoon programme is 30%+ lower than evening equivalent.
