## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a hospitality sector at an inflection point, caught between structural decline and strategic reinvention. The closure of all 106 Beefeater restaurants and the conversion of pubs at unprecedented rates signals not merely economic pressure, but a fundamental renegotiation of where and how people gather. Yet within this contraction, counter-movements emerge: Majestic Wine's £4 million investment in physical retail, Chapel Down's 19% sales growth, and the quiet resilience of wine auction markets suggest that experiential, expertise-led spaces retain cultural currency when they deliver genuine value.

The wine industry's struggles—Australian exports at 22-year lows, Bordeaux wildfires devastating tourism, global consumption at 1961 levels—are not simply category problems but reflections of broader consumer behaviour shifts. Younger drinkers are not abandoning alcohol entirely; they are demanding different relationships with it. The surge in "chilled reds" searches, Bordeaux's creation of a new "Claret" category for lighter wines served over ice, and the rise of RTD brands like Fellr targeting youth culture through music and surf collaborations all point toward a desire for approachability, flexibility, and lifestyle integration over traditional connoisseurship.

Meanwhile, the "third culture bakery" movement documented in London—Filipino-British, Palestinian-British, Middle Eastern-British fusions—represents a broader cultural appetite for hybrid identities and authentic personal narratives. This has direct implications for beverage brands: consumers increasingly seek products that tell stories of genuine cultural intersection rather than manufactured heritage.

The strategic imperative is clear: the future belongs to brands that can deliver expertise without pretension, cultural authenticity without exclusion, and physical experiences that justify their existence in an increasingly digital world.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Pub Conversion Acceleration

**What happened:** New data reveals pubs are now the most frequently converted building type in Britain, outpacing banks, churches, and post offices. Planning applications show systematic repurposing of hospitality spaces into residential and commercial properties.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the physical infrastructure of community gathering being systematically dismantled. The pub's role as "third place" is being architecturally erased, creating a vacuum in social infrastructure that alternative venues and experiences must fill.

**Societal layer:** Community / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Significant opportunity for non-alcoholic-forward venues to claim abandoned community gathering functions. The social need doesn't disappear with the building—it migrates. Brands that can facilitate new forms of local gathering, whether through pop-ups, partnerships with remaining venues, or entirely new formats, can capture displaced social energy.

**What to monitor:** Planning applications in specific postcodes; community response to closures; emergence of alternative gathering spaces (coffee shops extending hours, co-working spaces adding social programming).

### SIGNAL 2: Experiential Retail Defiance

**What happened:** Majestic Wine announced £4 million investment in physical stores, opening new locations and refurbishing existing ones, explicitly backing "experiential bricks and mortar" as central strategy. This follows 25+ store openings since 2019.

**Why it is a signal:** Against prevailing retail pessimism, this represents a bet that expertise-led, tactile retail experiences remain viable when they deliver genuine value. The emphasis on WSET-trained staff, free tastings, and wedding consultations positions stores as service hubs rather than mere transaction points.

**Societal layer:** Status / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands should pursue partnerships with experiential retailers who can provide the education and discovery context that complex NA beverages require. The "try before you buy" model is particularly relevant for premium NA products where taste profiles are unfamiliar.

**What to monitor:** Majestic's NA category expansion; emergence of NA-specialist retail concepts; consumer willingness to pay premium for guided discovery experiences.

### SIGNAL 3: The Chilled Reds Paradigm Shift

**What happened:** Google searches for "chilled reds" surged between February and June 2026, with queries shifting from "should I?" to "which ones?" Bordeaux has created an entirely new "Claret" category for light, chillable reds served over ice. Fortnum & Mason has curated a dedicated chilled reds collection.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the breakdown of traditional consumption rules and the rise of personal preference over prescribed behaviour. Consumers are actively seeking permission to drink differently, and institutions are responding by legitimising previously "incorrect" practices.

**Societal layer:** Rebellion / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages should embrace serving temperature flexibility as a feature, not a constraint. The broader signal is that consumers want to be told "drink it however you enjoy it" rather than receiving prescriptive instructions. This applies to glassware, occasion, and food pairing as well.

**What to monitor:** Social media content around "rule-breaking" beverage consumption; hospitality venues offering unconventional serving suggestions; consumer response to brands that explicitly reject traditional consumption norms.

### SIGNAL 4: Third Culture Culinary Identity

**What happened:** London's "third culture bakeries"—Filipino-British, Palestinian-British, Middle Eastern-British fusions—are gaining mainstream attention, with venues like Panadera, Bunhead, and Farha celebrated for blending family food traditions with contemporary British baking.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the mainstreaming of hyphenated identity as a source of cultural authority and commercial appeal. Authenticity is being redefined not as purity but as honest personal narrative and genuine cultural intersection.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands with genuine multicultural origin stories have significant opportunity. The key is specificity—not generic "global inspiration" but traceable personal and family narratives. Brands should consider how their founders' cultural backgrounds can inform product development and storytelling.

**What to monitor:** Consumer response to founder-story marketing; emergence of "third culture" beverage brands; restaurant and café partnerships that emphasise cultural fusion narratives.

### SIGNAL 5: Korean Gender Divergence in Drinking

**What happened:** Korean data shows high-risk drinking falling among men across all age groups over the past decade, while rising among women over 30. The shift is attributed to expanded social activities for women, diversification of drinking methods (including drinking alone at home), and changing social perceptions.

**Why it is a signal:** This reveals that moderation trends are not universal but demographically specific. Different populations are moving in different directions simultaneously, requiring nuanced rather than monolithic strategic responses.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands must avoid assuming uniform moderation trends. In some demographics, the opportunity is harm reduction; in others, it's providing sophisticated alternatives for newly empowered social drinkers. Market research must be granular enough to capture these divergences.

**What to monitor:** Demographic-specific drinking data across markets; emergence of gender-targeted beverage marketing; solo drinking culture evolution.

### SIGNAL 6: Youth RTD Culture Integration

**What happened:** Australian RTD brand Fellr, founded in a Bondi garage, has partnered with Australian Vintage for national distribution. The brand has built following through music, surf, fashion, and art collaborations, recently securing exclusive pours at six major stadiums.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the maturation of lifestyle-integrated beverage brands that build cultural credibility before pursuing scale. The stadium deals indicate mainstream acceptance of brands that originated in subculture.

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA RTD brands should prioritise cultural credibility-building through authentic subcultural partnerships before pursuing mass distribution. The Fellr model—garage origins, lifestyle collaborations, then stadium deals—provides a template for credibility-first scaling.

**What to monitor:** NA RTD brand partnerships with music festivals, surf competitions, and fashion events; stadium and venue NA RTD adoption; youth response to "corporate" versus "authentic" brand positioning.

### SIGNAL 7: Climate Crisis Hospitality Disruption

**What happened:** Bordeaux wildfires have forced evacuation of 220,000 people and devastated wine tourism—previously the only growing segment of the region's business. President Macron described it as France's toughest situation since WWII.

**Why it is a signal:** Climate events are no longer background risk but active disruptors of hospitality and tourism. The Bordeaux case demonstrates how quickly "bright spots" in struggling industries can be eliminated by environmental factors.

**Societal layer:** Control / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Climate resilience must become a strategic priority, not just a marketing message. Brands should assess supply chain vulnerability, consider geographic diversification, and develop contingency plans for climate-related disruption. Hospitality venues in climate-vulnerable areas need alternative revenue strategies.

**What to monitor:** Insurance costs for hospitality venues in climate-vulnerable regions; consumer response to climate-related supply disruptions; emergence of "climate-resilient" as a brand attribute.

### SIGNAL 8: Younger Vintage Acceleration

**What happened:** iDealwine reported Bordeaux wines under 10 years old represented 28% of auctioned bottles in 2025, up from 20% the previous year. The shift is attributed to changed winemaking styles that make wines accessible younger, reducing the appeal of "buy-and-wait" collecting.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a fundamental shift in the relationship between time and value. Consumers increasingly want immediate gratification rather than deferred pleasure, even in categories traditionally defined by patience and ageing.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should emphasise immediate enjoyment rather than acquired taste narratives. The "you'll learn to appreciate it" positioning is increasingly out of step with consumer expectations. Products should deliver pleasure on first encounter.

**What to monitor:** Consumer patience metrics across categories; success of "ready now" versus "develops over time" product positioning; generational differences in deferred gratification tolerance.

### SIGNAL 9: Sauna Heat Training Emergence

**What happened:** Scientists are studying whether repeated sauna use could help people cope better with heatwaves, as the UK experiences its fourth heatwave of the year. The research explores heat training as preparation for increasingly common extreme temperatures.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the normalisation of environmental adaptation as personal responsibility. Consumers are being encouraged to physically prepare their bodies for climate change, creating new wellness categories around resilience and adaptation.

**Societal layer:** Control / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Opportunity for beverages positioned around recovery, hydration, and heat adaptation. The sauna trend creates adjacent occasions for functional NA beverages. Hospitality venues incorporating heat experiences (saunas, hot yoga studios) represent partnership opportunities.

**What to monitor:** Sauna venue growth; "heat training" content on wellness platforms; functional beverage claims around thermoregulation and heat recovery.

### SIGNAL 10: Organic and Vegan Wine Mainstreaming

**What happened:** The Global Organic and Vegan Masters 2026 featured wines from across the globe, with judges noting that warmer, drier regions are finding organic viticulture increasingly viable. The competition reflects growing trade and consumer interest in sustainable wine production.

**Why it is a signal:** Organic and vegan are transitioning from niche positioning to baseline expectation, particularly in wine. The quality gap between conventional and organic production is closing, removing the traditional trade-off narrative.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Organic and vegan certification should be considered table stakes rather than differentiators for premium NA beverages. The strategic question is what comes after organic—regenerative, carbon-negative, or other emerging sustainability frameworks.

**What to monitor:** Consumer willingness to pay premium for organic NA beverages; emergence of post-organic sustainability certifications; retailer requirements for sustainability credentials.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Displaced Community Pop-Up Series

**Hypothesis:** Communities that have lost their local pub retain latent demand for third-place gathering that can be captured by NA-forward pop-up experiences in the same geographic areas.

**Method:** Identify 5 postcodes where pubs have been converted to residential in the past 18 months. Partner with remaining community spaces (village halls, libraries, community centres) to host monthly evening "social club" events featuring premium NA beverages, light food, and facilitated social activities. No alcohol served. Track attendance, repeat visits, and participant feedback on social connection.

**Success metric:** 60% repeat attendance rate; participant Net Promoter Score above 50; evidence of organic community formation (participants connecting outside events).

---

### Experiment 2: The Temperature Liberation Campaign

**Hypothesis:** Explicitly giving consumers permission to serve NA beverages however they prefer (temperature, glassware, occasion) will increase purchase intent and consumption frequency by removing perceived barriers to enjoyment.

**Method:** Develop packaging and marketing for an existing premium NA beverage that explicitly states "Serve however you enjoy it—chilled, room temperature, over ice, in any glass." Include QR code linking to content showing diverse serving styles. A/B test against control packaging with traditional serving suggestions. Measure purchase intent, consumption frequency, and social sharing of unconventional serving methods.

**Success metric:** 15% increase in purchase intent versus control; 25% increase in social media content showing product in non-traditional serving contexts; qualitative feedback indicating reduced anxiety about "correct" consumption.

---

### Experiment 3: The Third Culture Beverage Incubator

**Hypothesis:** NA beverages with genuine multicultural founder stories and ingredient combinations will outperform generically positioned competitors among consumers aged 25-40 in diverse urban markets.

**Method:** Partner with 3 founders from different cultural backgrounds to develop limited-edition NA beverages that blend ingredients and techniques from their heritage cultures with contemporary British beverage trends. Document founder stories through video content. Distribute through independent retailers in London, Manchester, and Birmingham. Track sales velocity, social engagement with founder content, and consumer willingness to pay premium.

**Success metric:** Sales velocity 30% above category average; founder story content engagement rate above 5%; consumer research indicating "authentic story" as primary purchase driver.
