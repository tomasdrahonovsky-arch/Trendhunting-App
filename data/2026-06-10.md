## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between nostalgia and reinvention. The drinks industry is experiencing a structural reset—not a cyclical downturn—as consumers fundamentally recalibrate their relationship with alcohol, ritual, and social experience. US alcohol consumption is declining more dramatically than anywhere else globally, forcing legacy players to pin hopes on events like the World Cup while simultaneously watching RTD cocktails surge to 80 million cases through familiar, uncomplicated formats rather than innovation.

The most significant pattern emerging is the death of novelty for novelty's sake. Whether it's RTD cocktails succeeding through "vodka and iced tea" simplicity, Singapore's cocktail scene pivoting from guest shifts to cross-disciplinary collaboration, or fine wine collectors demanding stories over labels, consumers are rejecting surface-level differentiation in favour of authenticity, provenance, and genuine human connection.

Caffeine has quietly become Gen Z's drug of choice—a psychoactive substance that fits their sober-curious identity while delivering functional benefits. This isn't anti-pleasure; it's pleasure recalibrated around productivity, wellness, and control. The wellness-industrial complex continues expanding, with whey protein shortages driven by GLP-1 drug users and orgasms being reframed as immune boosters.

Meanwhile, ancient practices are being rehabilitated for modern audiences: Alentejo's 2,000-year-old amphora wines, sorghum's gluten-free brewing potential, and regenerative viticulture all signal a hunger for pre-industrial authenticity. The care system's focus on reconnecting relationships and Vancouver's maple-leaf pride in local products both point toward community and belonging as primary consumer drivers.

For non-alcoholic beverage builders, the message is clear: stop chasing trends and start building genuine cultural infrastructure. The winners will be those who understand that today's consumer wants to feel something real, not just drink something new.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Simplicity Premium in RTD

**What happened:** US spirit-based RTDs grew by 10 million cases to nearly 80 million, but the gains came overwhelmingly from simple combinations—vodka and iced tea, spirit and seltzer, classic cocktail formats—not experimental flavours. Surfside (vodka + iced tea) grew 124% to become the second-largest RTD brand.

**Why it is a signal:** Consumers are rejecting complexity fatigue. After years of craft innovation and flavour proliferation, the market is rewarding legibility and familiarity. This represents a maturation from experimentation to repertoire—RTDs are no longer novelty purchases but habitual choices.

**Societal layer:** Control / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should resist the temptation to over-innovate. The opportunity lies in perfecting simple, recognisable formats—NA gin and tonic, NA aperitivo spritz, NA beer and lime—rather than inventing new categories. Familiar serves reduce purchase anxiety.

**What to monitor:** Which NA brands achieve repeat purchase rates comparable to alcoholic equivalents; consumer language around "my usual" in NA contexts.

### SIGNAL 2: Structural Reset, Not Cyclical Downturn

**What happened:** ASC Fine Wines' CEO declared China's wine market is experiencing a "structural reset, not a normal cyclical slowdown," while Rabobank analysts noted US alcohol consumption is "struggling more than virtually any other part of the world" with "dramatic declines not taking place elsewhere."

**Why it is a signal:** Industry leaders are publicly acknowledging that old assumptions have stopped working before new models are understood. This isn't temporary softness—it's a fundamental rewiring of how consumers relate to alcohol across the world's two largest markets.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The window for NA brands to establish category leadership is now. As alcohol incumbents struggle to understand the new landscape, NA players can define the rules. Focus on building infrastructure (distribution, on-premise presence, cultural legitimacy) rather than just products.

**What to monitor:** Major alcohol company earnings calls for language shifts; investment flows into NA versus alcoholic portfolios; on-premise tap handle allocation trends.

### SIGNAL 3: Caffeine as Gen Z's Sanctioned Stimulant

**What happened:** Dazed reports caffeine has become "Gen Z's drug of choice," with the UK coffee industry bigger than ever and expanding rapidly. This aligns with sober-curious wellness culture, where caffeine delivers functional benefits without the identity complications of alcohol.

**Why it is a signal:** Gen Z is not anti-substance—they're pro-control. Caffeine offers stimulation, ritual, and social participation without impairment or next-day consequences. It's a psychoactive substance that fits their productivity-oriented, wellness-conscious identity.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional NA beverages should lean into the caffeine-adjacent space—adaptogens, nootropics, and energy-without-crash formulations. The opportunity is creating "evening caffeine" equivalents that provide stimulation appropriate for social occasions without disrupting sleep.

**What to monitor:** Energy drink consumption patterns by occasion; emergence of "social caffeine" formats; coffee shop evening programming.

### SIGNAL 4: The Story Behind the Bottle

**What happened:** Justerini & Brooks reports Asian collectors are "no longer satisfied with just the label—they want to know the story behind the vineyard." Similarly, ASC Fine Wines notes the future belongs to producers who build "genuine consumer relationships through authenticity, terroir and storytelling."

**Why it is a signal:** Provenance has shifted from marketing differentiator to purchase prerequisite. Consumers are developing connoisseurship that demands narrative depth, not just quality claims. This represents a maturation from status consumption to informed appreciation.

**Societal layer:** Status / Identity / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands must invest in origin stories, production transparency, and founder narratives. The "craft" positioning requires substantiation. Consider terroir-driven NA products (specific botanical origins, water sources, regional ingredients) that reward consumer curiosity.

**What to monitor:** Consumer engagement with brand origin content; premium NA products that successfully command price premiums through storytelling; emergence of NA sommeliers and educators.

### SIGNAL 5: Ancient Methods, Modern Markets

**What happened:** Alentejo's 2,000-year-old talha (amphora) wine tradition is experiencing a renaissance, with producers reinterpreting the style for contemporary drinkers. Simultaneously, sorghum—an ancient grain—is being positioned as brewing's future for its gluten-free properties and sustainability credentials.

**Why it is a signal:** Pre-industrial production methods are being rehabilitated as authenticity markers. In a world of algorithmic optimisation, ancient techniques signal human craft, patience, and connection to place. This isn't nostalgia—it's a rejection of industrial homogeneity.

**Societal layer:** Spirituality / Identity / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Explore traditional fermentation methods (kvass, kombucha, shrubs, switchels) with genuine historical lineage. Position production time and method transparency as luxury markers. Consider partnerships with heritage producers or revival of regional NA traditions.

**What to monitor:** Consumer interest in fermentation education; growth of "slow beverage" positioning; success of heritage-method products in premium channels.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Community / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should position themselves as cultural collaborators, not just beverage providers. Partner with artists, musicians, and designers to create experiences where the drink is one element of a larger creative proposition. The drink becomes a ticket to participation.

**What to monitor:** Success metrics of cross-disciplinary hospitality events; emergence of NA brands as cultural sponsors; consumer willingness to pay premiums for experiential contexts.

### SIGNAL 7: Regenerative as Competitive Advantage

**What happened:** Alentejo wineries are implementing comprehensive sustainability programmes—cover crops, bat boxes, drone-monitored irrigation, cork oak reforestation—as climate change forces adaptation. These practices are becoming marketing differentiators, not just operational necessities.

**Why it is a signal:** Sustainability is transitioning from defensive positioning to offensive strategy. Producers who can demonstrate regenerative practices are building brand equity with consumers who increasingly view purchase decisions as ethical choices.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Spirituality / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands have an opportunity to lead on regenerative positioning, given their typically lower environmental footprint than alcohol production. Make sustainability claims specific and verifiable—carbon sequestration, water positivity, biodiversity metrics—not vague.

**What to monitor:** Consumer willingness to pay premiums for verified sustainability claims; emergence of regenerative certification standards for beverages; competitor sustainability commitments.

**What happened:** Whey protein prices have risen fivefold to record levels as GLP-1 weight-loss drug users are advised to increase protein consumption to preserve muscle mass. The pharmaceutical and wellness industries are converging around the same consumer.

**Why it is a signal:** Health interventions are creating new consumption patterns. The GLP-1 user represents a growing consumer segment with specific functional needs and high health consciousness—exactly the profile that indexes toward NA beverages.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Develop NA beverages with protein or amino acid profiles that complement GLP-1 protocols. Position NA options as part of holistic health regimens, not just alcohol alternatives. Consider partnerships with wellness practitioners and functional medicine providers.

**What to monitor:** GLP-1 prescription growth rates; emergence of "GLP-1 friendly" product positioning; protein content in beverage innovation pipelines.

### SIGNAL 9: Local Pride as Trade Response

**What happened:** Vancouver restaurants and bars have pulled American wines and spirits from shelves in response to tariffs, creating space for B.C. whisky, wines, and local products. Consumers are embracing "maple leaf pride" as both political statement and quality discovery.

**Why it is a signal:** Geopolitical tensions are reshaping beverage preferences. Consumers are finding that local alternatives often match or exceed imports, accelerating a localism trend that was already building. National identity is becoming a purchase driver.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should emphasise local production, regional ingredients, and national identity where authentic. In markets with trade tensions, "made here" positioning carries both patriotic and quality connotations. Build regional distribution before national.

**What to monitor:** Trade policy developments affecting beverage imports; consumer sentiment research on country-of-origin preferences; success of regional NA brands versus national/international competitors.

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Position NA venues and occasions as relationship infrastructure. Create programming specifically designed to facilitate connection—not just consumption. ### SIGNAL 11: Nostalgia as Activation Strategy

**What happened:** Pizza Hut's BOOK IT! button promotion—offering free pizza to anyone who brings their childhood reading reward button—sold out immediately. Bruichladdich's Yellow Submarine III whisky sold out before the launch event ended, with journalists receiving empty gift bags.

**Why it is a signal:** Nostalgia activations are generating genuine scarcity and urgency. Consumers are willing to engage with brands that acknowledge shared generational experiences. The empty gift bag is almost performance art—scarcity as brand statement.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Identify generational touchstones that NA brands can authentically reference. Consider limited releases tied to cultural moments or childhood memories. Scarcity marketing works when the underlying product has genuine cultural resonance.

**What to monitor:** Success of nostalgia-driven limited releases; consumer engagement with generational marketing; emergence of "heritage" positioning in NA category.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Simple Serve NA RTD Line

**Hypothesis:** NA RTD success will mirror alcoholic RTD patterns—simple, familiar formats will outperform innovative or complex offerings. An NA "vodka soda" equivalent (sparkling water with clean botanical notes) and NA "gin and tonic" format will achieve higher repeat purchase rates than novel flavour combinations.

**Method:** Develop three NA RTD SKUs: (1) a simple sparkling botanical water positioned as "NA vodka soda," (2) a tonic-forward bitter citrus serve positioned as "NA G&T," and (3) an experimental complex flavour as control. Launch in 50 on-premise accounts with identical pricing and placement. Track velocity, repeat purchase, and consumer language in feedback.

**Success metric:** Simple serves achieve 40% higher repeat purchase rate than complex flavour control within 90 days; consumer feedback includes language like "my usual" or "go-to" for simple serves.

---

### Experiment 2: The Regenerative Story Premium Test

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will pay a 25%+ premium for NA beverages with verified, specific regenerative claims (carbon sequestration metrics, water positivity, biodiversity impact) versus identical products with generic sustainability messaging.

**Method:** Create two versions of the same NA product: (1) with specific, third-party verified regenerative claims and detailed origin story, (2) with standard "sustainably produced" messaging. A/B test pricing elasticity in DTC channel, measuring conversion rates at various price points. Conduct post-purchase surveys on purchase drivers.

**Success metric:** Verified regenerative version maintains equivalent conversion at 25% price premium; post-purchase surveys show sustainability claims in top 3 purchase drivers for premium version.

---

### Experiment 3: The Cross-Disciplinary NA Social Club

**Hypothesis:** NA consumption will increase when positioned as one element of a broader creative/cultural experience rather than the primary offering. Consumers will pay premium prices and demonstrate higher engagement when NA beverages are integrated into art, music, or design programming.

**Method:** Partner with a gallery, music venue, or design studio to create a monthly "NA Social Club" event series. NA beverages are included in ticket price alongside creative programming (artist talks, listening sessions, design workshops). Track ticket sales, NA consumption per capita, social sharing, and repeat attendance versus control events (standard NA bar nights).

**Success metric:** NA Social Club events achieve 50% higher ticket prices, 30% higher NA consumption per capita, and 2x social sharing versus standard NA programming; 40% repeat attendance rate.
