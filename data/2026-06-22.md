## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a hospitality sector at an inflection point—caught between existential economic pressure and a renewed appetite for sensory maximalism. The UK on-trade faces what one commentator calls "an existential crisis," with restaurants and wine bars struggling under tax burdens while simultaneously representing irreplaceable social infrastructure. This tension matters because hospitality venues remain the primary theatre where beverage brands build meaning, ritual, and cultural relevance.

Simultaneously, we're witnessing a decisive return to theatrical drinking experiences. Foam cocktails are resurging across Asian bars as consumers demand "heightened, memorable experiences"—76% of Bacardi survey respondents now seek showstopping drinks full of glamour and theatrics. This isn't mere nostalgia; it's a post-pandemic recalibration where minimalism has exhausted its appeal and multisensory design becomes the new baseline expectation.

The provenance narrative continues to mature. English wines are shedding their "patriotic goodwill" crutch and earning genuine critical acclaim, while restaurants like Hélène Darroze at The Connaught restructure entire menus around ingredient origin stories. AB InBev's "ComBar"—a mobile bar built from a combine harvester—represents the logical extreme of this trend: making agricultural heritage physically spectacular.

For non-alcoholic beverages, the strategic imperative is clear. The category must graduate from "better-for-you" positioning into genuine experience design. Functional benefits alone won't capture the cultural moment. Brands that can deliver theatrical presentation, compelling provenance narratives, and venue-worthy rituals will capture the premiumisation opportunity. Those that remain purely transactional will find themselves competing on price in retail channels while missing the on-trade's meaning-making machinery entirely.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The On-Trade's Existential Reckoning

**What happened:** A passionate industry commentary argues that Britain's hospitality sector faces existential crisis, calling restaurants "the single greatest thing human beings have ever invented" and demanding VAT cuts to preserve the on-trade ecosystem.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a shift from viewing hospitality struggles as cyclical business challenges to framing them as cultural infrastructure under threat. The emotional register—defending the restaurant as "the campfire that learned to pour you a good glass of wine"—indicates the industry is mobilising around identity, not just economics.

**Societal layer:** Community / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands must actively invest in on-trade survival, not merely distribute through it. Brands that help venues create margin through premium NA offerings become partners in preservation rather than just suppliers.

**What to monitor:** UK hospitality VAT policy debates; independent venue closure rates; emergence of "support local hospitality" consumer movements.

### SIGNAL 2: Maximalism's Multisensory Return

**What happened:** Foam cocktails are resurging across Hong Kong, Bangkok, and Osaka bars, driven by consumer demand for theatrical, multisensory drinking experiences. The Bacardi 2026 Cocktail Trends Report found 76% of consumers seek "heightened, memorable experiences."

**Why it is a signal:** Post-pandemic minimalism has run its course. Consumers are actively rejecting restraint in favour of spectacle. The Netflix show "Drinks Masters" is cited as an accelerant—entertainment media is now directly shaping bar culture expectations.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages must develop theatrical serve rituals. Foams, layered presentations, tableside preparations, and dramatic glassware become essential—not optional—for premium positioning. The "Instagram moment" has evolved into the "full sensory moment."

**What to monitor:** Bartender competition formats; molecular mixology equipment sales; social media engagement rates on visually dramatic NA serves versus simple presentations.

### SIGNAL 3: Provenance as Menu Architecture

**What happened:** Hélène Darroze at The Connaught has restructured its entire à la carte menu around ingredient provenance, with dishes named by origin (e.g., "Crab – Davrik, Cornwall"). Menus will change with micro-seasons, and bespoke ceramics were commissioned to match the philosophy.

**Why it is a signal:** Provenance has graduated from marketing copy to structural organising principle. When a three-Michelin-starred restaurant rebuilds its entire menu architecture around origin stories, it signals that provenance is no longer a differentiator but a baseline expectation at the premium end.

**Societal layer:** Identity / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands must develop granular, verifiable provenance narratives—specific farms, specific harvests, specific makers. Generic "natural ingredients" claims will increasingly read as evasive. Consider menu integration where NA options are listed by origin alongside food.

**What to monitor:** Fine dining menu formats; sommelier attitudes toward NA provenance depth; consumer willingness to pay premiums for traceable ingredients.

**Societal layer:** Status / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The English wine trajectory offers a roadmap for NA premiumisation. Consistent competition performance, critical validation, and placement in prestigious venues can shift category perception over 5-10 years. NA brands should pursue blind tasting competitions and critical review infrastructure.

**What to monitor:** NA inclusion in established wine competitions; sommelier certification programmes adding NA modules; luxury hotel wine list NA representation.

### SIGNAL 5: Agricultural Spectacle Marketing

**Why it is a signal:** Provenance marketing is becoming experiential and spectacular. Static storytelling (labels, websites) is insufficient; brands are creating physical manifestations of their origin narratives that can travel and generate earned media.

**Societal layer:** Identity / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands with genuine agricultural stories should consider mobile, experiential activations that make provenance tangible. A botanical garden pop-up, a farm-based tasting room, or harvest-season events could create similar spectacle while building authenticity.

**What to monitor:** Experiential marketing ROI metrics in beverage; agricultural tourism trends; consumer engagement with "maker story" content versus product-focused content.

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages must develop daypart-spanning ranges. ### SIGNAL 7: Guinness as Social Experience Blueprint

**What happened:** Guinness launched limited-edition binoculars as collectibles, continuing a strategy that has made it the highest-grossing pint in British pubs. The brand's success is attributed to positioning the beer as "a social experience"—through pour rituals, glassware, and viral challenges like "Split the G"—rather than just a product.

**Why it is a signal:** Guinness demonstrates that ritual and social currency can drive category dominance even in a declining overall beer market. The brand has successfully made the drinking experience inseparable from the product itself.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands must develop ownable rituals and social challenges. The pour, the glass, the timing, the shareable moment—these elements can create cultural stickiness that transcends product attributes. Consider what the NA equivalent of "Split the G" might be.

**What to monitor:** Social media challenge engagement for beverage brands; ritual-focused marketing campaign performance; glassware and serve standardisation in NA.

**Why it is a signal:** Body awareness and functional health literacy are increasing. Consumers are becoming more sophisticated about how daily habits affect physical wellbeing, creating receptivity to products positioned around functional benefits—but with credible, specific mechanisms.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Ritual Development Lab

**Hypothesis:** NA beverages with codified, shareable serving rituals will achieve higher on-trade velocity and social media engagement than those served without ritual, mirroring Guinness's success with pour culture.

**Method:** Partner with 10 cocktail bars across three cities to develop and test three distinct NA serving rituals (e.g., a specific pour technique, a tableside finishing step, a signature glassware moment). Train bartenders, provide ritual cards, and track both sales velocity and user-generated social content featuring the ritual over 12 weeks. Compare against control venues serving the same products without ritual emphasis.

**Success metric:** 25% higher rate of sale in ritual venues versus control; 3x increase in tagged social media content featuring the ritual; bartender Net Promoter Score above 40 for ritual products.

---

### Experiment 2: Provenance Menu Integration

**Hypothesis:** NA beverages listed on menus with specific provenance information (farm, region, maker) alongside food items will command 15-20% price premiums and higher selection rates than those listed generically in a separate "non-alcoholic" section.

**Method:** Work with five restaurants to redesign menu sections, integrating NA options within the main beverage list with provenance-forward descriptions matching the food menu's origin-focused language. A/B test menu formats over 8 weeks, tracking selection rates, average transaction value, and customer feedback on perceived value.

**Success metric:** 15% increase in NA selection rate; 20% higher average price point achieved; qualitative feedback indicating NA perceived as "part of the meal" rather than "alternative."

---

### Experiment 3: The Daypart Range Test

**Hypothesis:** Venues offered a coherent NA range spanning morning-to-evening occasions (energising morning option, refreshing afternoon option, complex evening option) will capture more NA occasions per customer visit than venues with single-occasion NA offerings.

**Method:** Develop a three-SKU "daypart collection" with distinct flavour profiles and energy levels. Place in 15 all-day venues (cafés transitioning to bars, hotel lobbies, co-working spaces with evening programming). Track NA purchases per unique customer, time-of-day distribution, and repeat purchase patterns over 10 weeks.

**Success metric:** Average of 1.4 NA occasions per customer visit (versus industry baseline of 1.0); even distribution across dayparts (no single daypart exceeding 50% of sales); 30% of customers purchasing from multiple dayparts within observation period.
