## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a tension between resilience and fragmentation—communities finding ways to persist through adversity while simultaneously seeking new frameworks for identity, pleasure, and belonging. The drinks industry mirrors this duality: Lebanese winemakers continue producing under literal bombardment while English wine experiences a fivefold increase in trade representation; ancient Spanish vineyards face extinction not from climate but from solar subsidies; and a Chinese beer brand designed for communal hotpot dining expands into Malaysia, riding the wave of diaspora cuisine culture.

The most significant undercurrent is the recalibration of what constitutes "effort" in relationships and consumption. The viral "Plastic Bag Theory" critiques partners who appear easygoing but are actually disengaged—a metaphor that extends to how consumers now evaluate brands. Authenticity demands active participation, not passive presence. Champagne houses speak of innovation as tradition; Wetherspoon opens in London's West End with heritage-inspired naming; and even depression detection has been gamified into a three-minute phone experience.

For non-alcoholic beverages, the opportunity lies in the spaces between these tensions. Consumers are seeking drinks that facilitate genuine connection (not just social lubrication), that carry provenance stories worth telling, and that respect the intelligence of drinkers who increasingly understand that "lower ABV" doesn't mean "lower experience." The Circle 13 cherry kalimotxo—a lower-ABV highball designed for relaxed petanque evenings—exemplifies this: sophisticated enough to satisfy, accessible enough to share, rooted in place and purpose.

The cultural moment demands beverages that do something—that participate actively in the occasion rather than simply being present. Passive refreshment is no longer enough.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: Resilience as Brand Narrative

**What happened:** Chateau Musar's winemaker discussed producing world-class wine amid Israeli airstrikes, with neighbouring villages bombed while maintaining organic certification and philosophical integrity.

**Why it is a signal:** Consumers increasingly value brands that persist through genuine adversity rather than manufactured struggle narratives. Authenticity now requires demonstrated resilience, not just claimed heritage.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Origin stories must evolve beyond terroir to include the human cost and commitment behind production. Brands with genuine adversity narratives (conflict zones, climate extremes, economic hardship) carry emotional weight that comfortable provenance cannot match.

**What to monitor:** Consumer response to "difficulty narratives" in marketing; premium pricing tolerance for products from challenged regions; emergence of "solidarity purchasing" as a category driver.

---

### SIGNAL 2: Innovation as Tradition Defence

**What happened:** Champagne De Saint-Gall's cellar master argued that innovation is inherent to Champagne's history, positioning climate adaptation and pesticide reduction as continuations of tradition rather than departures from it.

**Why it is a signal:** Premium categories are reframing necessary change as heritage preservation, resolving the tension between evolution and authenticity that paralyses many legacy brands.

**Societal layer:** Status / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands can position themselves as the next logical step in beverage evolution—not a rejection of drinking culture but its maturation. Frame innovation as honouring the craft tradition of "making better drinks."

**What to monitor:** How established alcohol brands discuss their NA extensions; consumer acceptance of "evolution narratives" versus "disruption narratives"; trade press framing of category innovation.

---

### SIGNAL 3: Subsidy-Driven Cultural Erasure

**What happened:** Spain's oldest Tempranillo vineyards in D.O. Toro face destruction not from climate change but from solar panel subsidies that make energy production more profitable than grape-growing.

**Why it is a signal:** Economic incentives are now actively competing with cultural preservation, creating unexpected threats to heritage products. The market alone cannot protect cultural assets.

**Societal layer:** Community / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands built on agricultural heritage must consider policy environments, not just climate. There's an opportunity to position certain products as "cultural preservation purchases" where consumer choice actively protects endangered traditions.

**What to monitor:** Agricultural subsidy shifts in key growing regions; emergence of "heritage protection" certifications; consumer willingness to pay premiums for culturally endangered ingredients.

---

### SIGNAL 4: Cuisine-Led Beverage Expansion

**What happened:** Carlsberg's ChongQing beer launched in Malaysia specifically to pair with the booming Chinese restaurant sector, with Chinese-owned restaurants growing from 1,000 to 15,000+ in two decades.

**Why it is a signal:** Beverage expansion is following cuisine migration patterns rather than traditional distribution logic. Diaspora food culture creates ready-made occasions and pairing expectations.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Map non-alcoholic beverage development to emerging cuisine trends. As specific food cultures gain mainstream traction (Korean, regional Chinese, West African), develop drinks designed explicitly for those flavour profiles and dining occasions.

**What to monitor:** Restaurant category growth by cuisine type; food delivery platform trending cuisines; social media food content by origin; diaspora population shifts in key markets.

### SIGNAL 5: The "Plastic Bag" Critique of Passive Engagement

**What happened:** The "Plastic Bag Theory" went viral, criticising partners who appear easygoing but are actually passive and low-effort, placing unfair burdens on others to drive the relationship.

**Why it is a signal:** Cultural patience for passive participation is exhausted. Whether in relationships or consumption, "going with the flow" is being reframed as a failure to show up authentically.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands must actively participate in occasions, not just be present. This means drinks with purpose, ritual, or conversation-starting qualities. The "whatever you want" beverage is now suspect—consumers want drinks that have a point of view.

**What to monitor:** Consumer language around brand "personality" and "effort"; social media critique of "basic" or "safe" choices; premium growth in opinionated, distinctive products versus crowd-pleasers.

---

### SIGNAL 6: Gamified Mental Health Detection

**What happened:** NYU researchers developed a three-minute phone game that detects depression by measuring how quickly players abandon diminishing-return tasks, with depressed users leaving productive situations earlier.

**Why it is a signal:** Mental health assessment is being embedded into everyday digital interactions, normalising continuous self-monitoring and creating new expectations for products that "know" the user's state.

**Societal layer:** Mental Health / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional beverages may eventually integrate with mood-tracking ecosystems, suggesting drinks based on detected states. More immediately, brands should consider how their products support mental health without making clinical claims.

**What to monitor:** Integration of mood detection into consumer apps; regulatory frameworks for health-adjacent beverage claims; consumer comfort with algorithmic beverage recommendations.

### SIGNAL 7: Heritage Naming as Place-Making

**What happened:** Wetherspoon's first West End venue will be called "Piccadilly Hall," referencing the 17th-century tailor whose "piccadill" collars gave Piccadilly Circus its name, with design elements echoing the collar shape.

**Why it is a signal:** Even mass-market hospitality brands are investing in deep heritage narratives to create sense of place. Generic branding is losing ground to historically-rooted storytelling.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venue-specific or region-specific non-alcoholic drinks with genuine historical connections can command attention and premium positioning. The story must be real, researched, and specific—not manufactured.

**What to monitor:** Consumer engagement with heritage narratives in hospitality; success of location-specific menu items; social media sharing of "origin story" content.

### SIGNAL 8: Lower-ABV as Lifestyle Enabler

**What happened:** Circle 13's cherry kalimotxo was featured as a "lower-ABV highball for relaxed evenings of petanque," explicitly positioning reduced alcohol as enabling longer, more social occasions.

**Why it is a signal:** The framing has shifted from "drinking less" (restriction) to "staying present longer" (enhancement). Lower ABV is becoming a feature for better experiences, not a compromise.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Position non-alcoholic options as enabling extended social occasions—the drink that lets you stay sharp for the third hour of the dinner party, the nightcap that doesn't compromise tomorrow's morning run.

**What to monitor:** Language used to describe moderation choices; occasion length data for lower-ABV versus full-strength consumption; emergence of "session" positioning in premium categories.

### SIGNAL 9: English Wine's Trade Legitimacy Moment

**What happened:** London Wine Fair saw a fivefold increase in English wine producers exhibiting, with all five most-searched producers being English, and a dedicated "Host Nation" initiative driving trade interest.

**Why it is a signal:** English wine has crossed from novelty to serious trade consideration, creating a template for how emerging wine regions—and by extension, emerging beverage categories—gain professional credibility.

**Societal layer:** Status / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic beverages should study English wine's path to legitimacy: trade fair presence, sommelier education, restaurant placement, and critical recognition. The playbook exists; the category must execute it.

**What to monitor:** Non-alcoholic representation at major trade fairs; sommelier certification programmes including NA content; fine dining NA pairing menu adoption rates.

---

### SIGNAL 10: Screen Time Quality Differentiation

**What happened:** The Guardian published guidance on "how to quit doomscrolling without quitting your phone," emphasising that not all screen time is equal—learning a language differs from scrolling TikTok.

**Why it is a signal:** Consumers are developing more sophisticated frameworks for evaluating their own consumption, distinguishing between "empty" and "nourishing" versions of the same activity.

**Societal layer:** Control / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** This quality-differentiation framework will extend to beverages. Consumers will increasingly distinguish between "empty refreshment" and drinks that offer something more—functional benefits, craft appreciation, social facilitation, or genuine pleasure.

**What to monitor:** Consumer language around "quality" consumption; growth in beverages with explicit functional or experiential positioning; decline of "just a drink" occasions.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The "Active Presence" Menu Positioning Test

**Hypothesis:** Describing non-alcoholic drinks in terms of what they enable (extended conversation, sharper presence, morning-after clarity) rather than what they lack (alcohol) will increase selection rates and willingness to pay.

**Method:** Partner with three hospitality venues to A/B test menu descriptions for identical NA drinks. Control menus use standard descriptors ("alcohol-free sparkling wine"). Test menus use enablement language ("the drink that keeps you sharp through dessert"). Track selection rates, price sensitivity, and post-purchase satisfaction over 8 weeks.

**Success metric:** 25% increase in NA selection rate; 15% higher average spend on NA options; qualitative feedback indicating reduced perception of compromise.

---

### Experiment 2: Cuisine-Paired NA Development Sprint

**Hypothesis:** Non-alcoholic beverages designed explicitly for specific cuisine pairings (Korean BBQ, Sichuan hotpot, West African stews) will outperform generic NA options in relevant restaurant contexts.

**Method:** Develop three cuisine-specific NA beverages with flavour profiles designed to complement (not just accompany) the target cuisine. Partner with restaurants in each cuisine category for exclusive 12-week trials. Measure reorder rates, customer feedback, and comparison against existing NA options.

**Success metric:** 40% higher reorder rate than generic NA alternatives; inclusion on permanent menus at 2+ partner restaurants; documented flavour-pairing rationale that can scale to other cuisines.

---

### Experiment 3: The "Resilience Provenance" Premium Test

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will pay a measurable premium for beverages with genuine adversity narratives (conflict zones, climate extremes, economic hardship) compared to comfortable-provenance alternatives.

**Method:** Source or develop two comparable NA products—one with standard premium provenance, one with documented resilience narrative (e.g., ingredients from climate-challenged regions, production supporting communities in difficulty). Present both at identical quality positioning but test price elasticity and purchase rationale through direct-to-consumer and on-premise channels.

**Success metric:** 20% price premium tolerance for resilience-narrative product; qualitative research showing emotional engagement with story; repeat purchase rate indicating narrative isn't just novelty.
