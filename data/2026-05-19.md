## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between acceleration and intentionality. Across disparate domains—from psychedelic therapy for the ultra-wealthy to frost-fighting techniques in Canadian vineyards—we observe a consistent pattern: people are seeking depth, provenance, and emotional resonance over speed and spectacle.

The non-alcoholic beverage space is maturing rapidly. Beau Viva's partnership with Paradiso's Gabriele Armani signals that premium NA drinks have crossed a critical threshold—they're now worthy of serious mixological attention from the world's best bartenders. His statement that "mindful drinking was never supposed to mean drinking less beautifully" encapsulates the sector's evolution from deprivation narrative to genuine luxury positioning.

Meanwhile, the celebrity "taste signalling" phenomenon documented by Dazed—where cultural figures broadcast their consumption choices via Letterboxd and book clubs—reflects a broader shift toward identity construction through curation rather than creation. This has direct implications for beverage brands: what you drink is increasingly a statement about who you are, not merely what you enjoy.

The food desert research from rural Britain and the NHS artificial pancreas rollout both point to growing awareness of health equity—who has access to wellness, and who doesn't. This consciousness will inevitably shape expectations around functional beverages and their distribution.

Perhaps most striking is the emergence of professional "tripsitters" for wealthy psychedelic users, representing the ultimate commodification of consciousness exploration. This signals that experiences once considered countercultural are being absorbed into premium service economies—a trajectory non-alcoholic and functional beverage brands should study carefully.

The through-line: consumers want transformation, not just consumption. They want drinks that mean something, experiences that change them, and choices that signal their values.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Professionalisation of Consciousness

**What happened:** A cottage industry of "tripsitters" has emerged to guide ultra-wealthy clients through high-dose psychedelic experiences, with figures like Bryan Johnson livestreaming 5-MeO-DMT sessions to millions.

**Why it is a signal:** The commodification of consciousness exploration—once the domain of counterculture—into premium service experiences indicates that transformation itself has become a luxury good. The wealthy are paying not just for substances but for curated, safe journeys through altered states.

**Societal layer:** Spirituality / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional beverages positioned around mood modulation, focus, or relaxation must consider the "guide" element. The product alone is insufficient; the experience architecture matters. Premium NA brands could develop "ritual guides" or partnerships with wellness practitioners to elevate consumption into ceremony.

**What to monitor:** Growth of integration coaches, retreat pricing trends, mainstream media coverage of microdosing protocols, and regulatory shifts around psilocybin in hospitality settings.

### SIGNAL 2: Emotional Architecture in Travel Retail

**What happened:** Brown Forman's Glendrionach House Editions repositions travel retail whisky around "rooms" and emotional states rather than technical specifications, creating an interior-design-meets-spirits narrative.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a shift from product-centric to experience-centric storytelling in a traditionally transactional environment. The focus on "warmth and homeliness" acknowledges that stressed travellers seek emotional regulation, not just duty-free savings.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Mental health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Travel retail represents an underexplored frontier for premium NA beverages. Positioning around stress reduction, arrival rituals, or "transition states" (home-to-away) could capture travellers seeking alternatives to alcohol. The emotional framing—not the functional claims—will drive conversion.

**What to monitor:** Travel retail category expansion for NA, airport wellness lounge concepts, and airline beverage programme changes.

### SIGNAL 3: Bartender Legitimacy for NA Spirits

**What happened:** Beau Viva partnered with Gabriele Armani of Paradiso (consistently ranked among the world's best bars) to develop four mocktail recipes, with Armani describing the NA sparkling rosé as "a canvas with genuine depth."

**Why it is a signal:** When elite bartenders publicly endorse NA products as worthy of their craft—rather than as compromise solutions—the category achieves a new level of cultural legitimacy. Armani's framing of NA as "a new language" rather than adaptation signals creative parity.

**Societal layer:** Status / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Bartender advocacy is now the most credible form of NA brand building. Investment in bartender education, co-creation programmes, and competition sponsorships will yield higher returns than traditional marketing. The "made for mixing" positioning requires genuine complexity.

**What to monitor:** NA categories in major cocktail competitions, bartender-founded NA brands, and on-premise NA menu sophistication.

### SIGNAL 4: Taste as Identity Infrastructure

**What happened:** Dazed documented the rise of celebrity "taste signalling"—Charli xcx on Letterboxd, Dua Lipa's book club, artists sharing playlists—as a primary mode of public identity construction.

**Why it is a signal:** Consumption choices have become the raw material of identity. What you watch, read, and drink now functions as social currency and tribal marker. This shifts beverage marketing from persuasion to affiliation—brands must be "discoverable" within taste ecosystems.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should invest in cultural adjacencies—film, music, literature—rather than traditional beverage marketing. Appearing on the "right" lists, in the "right" contexts, matters more than reach. Consider partnerships with tastemaker platforms and cultural curators.

**What to monitor:** Beverage mentions in cultural recommendation platforms, influencer "what I consume" content formats, and cross-category brand collaborations.

### SIGNAL 5: Rural Food Deserts and Access Inequality

**What happened:** University of Sheffield research revealed that over half of rural UK households earning under £40,000 struggle to access affordable, healthy food, with the Cotswolds identified as a "food desert" despite its affluent image.

**Why it is a signal:** Health and wellness access is becoming a visible class marker. The gap between those who can easily obtain nutritious options and those who cannot is widening, creating both social tension and market opportunity for brands that solve distribution challenges.

**Societal layer:** Control / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional beverages positioned as health solutions must grapple with distribution equity. Brands that solve last-mile challenges in underserved areas—through mobile retail, subscription models, or community partnerships—will build authentic purpose credentials.

**What to monitor:** Government food access initiatives, community shop models, mobile retail innovations, and health equity discourse in beverage marketing.

### SIGNAL 6: The Palestinian Terroir Emergence

**What happened:** Franco-Palestinian chef Fadi Kattan and sommelier Anna Patrowicz are introducing four Palestinian wine producers to the London Wine Fair, explicitly rejecting pity-based purchasing in favour of quality-based evaluation.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the maturation of "political consumption" from solidarity purchasing to genuine quality recognition. Kattan's insistence that wines "speak for themselves" signals a shift from virtue signalling to substantive engagement with marginalised producers.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Origin stories from conflict regions or marginalised communities can drive differentiation, but only when paired with genuine quality. The "don't buy because you feel bad" framing offers a template for authentic purpose positioning that respects consumer intelligence.

**What to monitor:** Emerging wine/beverage regions with geopolitical narratives, consumer response to origin-based marketing, and sommelier/buyer attitudes toward "story-driven" products.

### SIGNAL 7: ABV-Based Taxation Arrives

**What happened:** Karnataka became the first Indian state to implement alcohol-by-volume taxation, replacing arbitrary price slabs. Mild beers dropped 20-25% in price while stronger drinks became relatively more expensive.

**Why it is a signal:** Tax policy shapes consumption patterns. ABV-based taxation incentivises lower-strength options and creates structural advantages for session-strength and non-alcoholic beverages. This model may spread to other markets.

**Societal layer:** Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Monitor regulatory environments for ABV-based taxation adoption. In markets where this occurs, NA and low-ABV products gain price competitiveness. Brands should engage with policy discussions and prepare market entry strategies for regions adopting similar frameworks.

**What to monitor:** Tax policy discussions in key markets, WHO recommendations on alcohol taxation, and price elasticity data from Karnataka.

### SIGNAL 8: Japanese Alcohol's Korean Renaissance

**What happened:** Japanese alcohol imports to South Korea hit record highs in Q1 2026, up 517% since 2019, driven by the weak yen, highball culture, and Japan travel enthusiasm—completely reversing the 2019 "No Japan" boycott.

**Why it is a signal:** Cultural boycotts can be remarkably temporary when economic and experiential factors align. The "highball boom" demonstrates how serve format innovation can revive entire categories. Consumer politics are more fluid than assumed.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Serve format innovation (the highball model) can transform category perception. NA brands should study how Japanese producers rebuilt Korean market share through experiential positioning—izakaya culture, omakase dining—rather than direct product marketing.

**What to monitor:** Cross-cultural beverage trends in Asia, serve format innovations, and the relationship between travel patterns and beverage preferences.

### SIGNAL 9: The British Pub as Nostalgia Technology

**What happened:** Dean's, a new British pub in NYC from the team behind King, opened with traditional dishes like stargazy pie and house-made crumpets, positioning heritage British cuisine as worthy of serious culinary attention.

**Why it is a signal:** Nostalgia for "authentic" cultural experiences is driving hospitality concepts that would have seemed unfashionable a decade ago. The British pub—complete with Guinness oyster chasers—represents comfort, ritual, and cultural specificity in an era of homogenised experiences.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Heritage and tradition are underexploited positioning territories for NA beverages. Brands that can credibly claim historical roots or traditional production methods gain authenticity advantages. Consider how NA options can integrate into ritual-heavy hospitality concepts.

**What to monitor:** Heritage hospitality concept openings, consumer nostalgia research, and traditional beverage format revivals.

### SIGNAL 10: Dogs as Social Friction Point

**What happened:** The Guardian documented growing tension around dogs in public spaces—restaurants, offices, flights—with incidents including a dachshund on a training pad in a dining room, highlighting the collision between pet culture and shared space norms.

**Why it is a signal:** The boundaries of acceptable public behaviour are being renegotiated in real-time. **Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Hospitality venues must navigate pet policies carefully. **What to monitor:** Pet policy debates in hospitality, dog-friendly venue growth, and consumer segmentation around pet attitudes.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Ritual Guide Programme

**Hypothesis:** Partnering with wellness practitioners to create guided consumption rituals for functional NA beverages will increase perceived value, repeat purchase intent, and willingness to pay premium prices by at least 25% compared to product-only positioning.

**Method:** Develop a pilot programme with 3-5 meditation teachers, breathwork facilitators, or wellness coaches to create 10-minute "ritual guides" (audio/video) paired with a functional NA beverage. Test with 200 consumers in a controlled setting, measuring perceived value, emotional response, and purchase intent against a control group receiving product only. Track 30-day repeat purchase behaviour.

**Success metric:** 25%+ increase in willingness to pay; 30%+ increase in repeat purchase intent; qualitative feedback indicating emotional connection to consumption experience.

---

### Experiment 2: Bartender Co-Creation Accelerator

**Hypothesis:** Involving top bartenders in product development from formulation stage—not just recipe creation post-launch—will produce NA beverages with significantly higher on-premise adoption rates and trade advocacy.

**Method:** Recruit 5 bartenders from top-50 global bars to participate in a 6-month co-creation process for a new NA spirit, including flavour development, naming, and positioning. Compare on-premise placement rates, bartender recommendation frequency, and social media advocacy against a comparable NA product developed through traditional R&D processes.

**Success metric:** 40%+ higher on-premise placement rate in target accounts; measurable increase in unsolicited bartender social media mentions; trade press coverage citing bartender involvement.

---

### Experiment 3: The Underserved Market Mobile Concept

**Hypothesis:** A mobile NA beverage retail concept targeting rural "food desert" communities will generate meaningful sales while building authentic brand purpose credentials that translate to premium positioning in urban markets.

**Method:** Launch a 3-month pilot mobile retail unit visiting 10 rural UK communities identified as food deserts, offering curated NA beverages alongside basic wellness products. Track sales data, community response, and media coverage. Simultaneously measure brand perception shifts among urban consumers exposed to the initiative through owned and earned media.

**Success metric:** Positive unit economics within 3 months; measurable brand perception improvement in urban target demographic; at least 2 pieces of earned media coverage in mainstream outlets.
