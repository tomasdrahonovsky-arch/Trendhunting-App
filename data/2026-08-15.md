## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between visibility and retreat, authenticity and performance, institutional failure and grassroots resilience. The most significant pattern emerging across these signals is what might be termed "selective presence"—a deliberate curation of when, how, and to whom we reveal ourselves.

Gen Z's "ghost posting" on Instagram represents a fundamental rejection of the always-on performance economy that defined millennial social media use. This isn't digital detox; it's strategic invisibility—posting for the archive, not the audience. Simultaneously, we see communities forming around shared identity markers that institutions have failed to serve: transmasculine football clubs creating physical and emotional homes, Indigenous Gen Z punks in Mexico City building alternative economies of style and belonging, queer photographers documenting "dirt and beauty" as equally valid aesthetic categories.

The earthwork dining movement signals a deeper hunger—not just for food, but for experiences that collapse the boundaries between consumption, art, and nature. When diners gather around a waterfall to eat vegetable purées painted directly onto rock, they're seeking something that no restaurant can provide: a return to pre-industrial modes of communal eating that feel transgressive in their simplicity.

Meanwhile, institutional care continues to fracture. Soup kitchens face closure for attracting "crowds of vulnerable people"—the very population they exist to serve. Parent carers of disabled children report systems that fail to care. Older workers find themselves unemployable despite decades of experience. These failures create vacuums that alternative communities and experiences rush to fill.

For beverage brands, the opportunity lies in understanding that consumers increasingly seek products and experiences that facilitate selective presence—drinks that signal belonging to specific communities, rituals that create intentional moments of connection, and brands that acknowledge the exhaustion of constant performance.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: Ghost Posting and Strategic Invisibility

**What happened:** Gen Z users are increasingly "ghost posting" on Instagram—uploading content, immediately archiving it, then selectively unarchiving later. Users describe posting "in secret" as liberating, acknowledging that content "won't be seen by a lot of people" and finding freedom in that constraint.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a fundamental shift from social media as broadcast medium to social media as personal archive. The performance economy that defined 2010s Instagram is being actively rejected by its youngest users, who prefer selective revelation over constant visibility.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands should reconsider always-on social strategies. Consider "hidden" product drops, unlisted menu items, or experiences that reward those who seek them out rather than those who stumble upon them. The anti-algorithm aesthetic has commercial potential.

**What to monitor:** Archive/unarchive ratios among Gen Z users; growth of "finsta" culture; emergence of platforms designed for selective sharing; decline in public posting frequency among 18-25 demographic.

### SIGNAL 2: Earthwork Dining and Radical Commensality

**What happened:** Chef Charles Michel hosted a dinner where food was painted directly onto rocks beside a waterfall—no tables, chairs, or plates. Guests experienced "collective pause" and "childlike playfulness," scooping purées with bread. This represents a growing movement of "earthwork dining" that treats meals as land art.

**Why it is a signal:** This isn't merely experiential dining; it's a rejection of the entire apparatus of hospitality infrastructure. The removal of plates, tables, and chairs forces a return to pre-civilizational eating modes, suggesting deep dissatisfaction with how contemporary dining mediates our relationship with food and each other.

**Societal layer:** Spirituality / Community / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Beverage experiences could explore radical simplicity—drinks served from natural vessels, consumed in nature without glassware, or rituals that require physical proximity and shared containers. The "relational aesthetic" framework offers rich territory for drink-as-social-sculpture.

**What to monitor:** Growth of outdoor dining experiences that eliminate traditional service elements; emergence of "foraging sommeliers"; consumer interest in drinks served from non-traditional vessels; retreat and wellness programming that incorporates communal eating.

### SIGNAL 3: Identity-Based Athletic Communities

**What happened:** Homme FC, a transmasculine football club in London, has developed "Homme Apartment"—a photo series imagining what the team might feel like as a physical space. The project asks: "How can we reinscribe ourselves into our past?" The club began as a WhatsApp group and has evolved into a creative and athletic community.

**Why it is a signal:** When traditional institutions fail to provide belonging, communities create their own. The evolution from WhatsApp group to football club to conceptual art project demonstrates how identity-based communities are building parallel infrastructures that serve multiple needs simultaneously—athletic, social, creative, and political.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Sponsorship and partnership opportunities exist with emerging identity-based athletic communities that mainstream sports brands may overlook. These communities value authenticity and long-term commitment over transactional relationships. Consider creating beverages specifically formulated for these communities' needs.

**What to monitor:** Growth of identity-specific sports leagues and clubs; emergence of creative projects from athletic communities; WhatsApp and Discord groups evolving into physical gatherings; demand for inclusive athletic spaces.

### SIGNAL 4: Wellness Cult Aesthetics Go Mainstream

**What happened:** FKA twigs partnered with athletic brand On for an immersive experience in Seoul, where guests wore identical branded clothing with her "Eusexua" logo, sat on individual cushions in a repurposed oil tank, and participated in what the journalist explicitly compared to the Rajneesh cult from "Wild Wild Country."

**Why it is a signal:** The cult aesthetic—once a warning sign—has become aspirational. Consumers are actively seeking experiences that demand surrender, uniformity, and submission to a charismatic vision. This represents a hunger for structure and meaning that secular consumer culture has failed to provide.

**Societal layer:** Spirituality / Belonging / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** There's appetite for beverage experiences that demand commitment—membership programs with rituals, dress codes, or behavioral expectations. The "wellness cult" aesthetic can be deployed without actual manipulation, offering the form of devotion without its dangers.

**What to monitor:** Growth of membership-based wellness experiences; consumer willingness to adopt uniforms or dress codes; emergence of charismatic brand founders as quasi-spiritual figures; retreat programming that emphasizes surrender and structure.

### SIGNAL 5: Institutional Care Failure Creates Grassroots Response

**What happened:** Westminster Council is threatening to close a soup kitchen because it attracts "crowds of vulnerable people." Simultaneously, reporting on parent carers reveals systems that "fail to care," with families needing support "before exhaustion becomes a crisis." These represent parallel failures of institutional care.

**Why it is a signal:** When institutions designed to care actively reject those who need care, communities must build alternatives. This creates both crisis and opportunity—the vacuum left by failing institutions is being filled by grassroots organizations, mutual aid networks, and community-based solutions.

**Societal layer:** Community / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands can support and partner with grassroots care organizations without co-opting their work. Consider "pay it forward" programs, partnerships with mutual aid networks, or creating spaces that welcome populations excluded from mainstream hospitality.

**What to monitor:** Growth of mutual aid networks; emergence of community-based care alternatives; consumer sentiment toward brands that support vulnerable populations; regulatory pressure on grassroots care organizations.

### SIGNAL 6: Generational Contract Rupture

**What happened:** Guardian columnist Polly Toynbee, writing for nearly 50 years, explicitly states she's "not on her own generation's side" and that the social contract between generations has been broken. Research shows 40% of 25-year-olds now live with parents, facing worse prospects than any generation in half a century.

**Why it is a signal:** When establishment voices explicitly side against their own demographic cohort, it signals a rupture in generational solidarity. This creates permission for younger generations to reject inherited frameworks—including consumption patterns, career expectations, and lifestyle aspirations.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Rebellion / Status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Younger consumers may reject aspirational marketing that assumes traditional life milestones (homeownership, career advancement) are achievable. Brands should acknowledge economic reality rather than selling fantasy. Products positioned as "affordable luxury" or "accessible indulgence" may resonate.

**What to monitor:** Shifts in aspirational marketing effectiveness among under-35 consumers; growth of "anti-hustle" content; consumer rejection of traditional status markers; emergence of new status symbols appropriate to constrained economic circumstances.

### SIGNAL 7: Antihistamines as Hormone Hacks

**What happened:** Women on social media are claiming that over-the-counter antihistamines (Pepcid AC and Allegra) help manage PMDD and menopause symptoms. This represents grassroots pharmaceutical experimentation driven by inadequate medical care for women's hormonal health.

**Why it is a signal:** When formal healthcare fails to address common conditions, consumers turn to peer-to-peer knowledge sharing and off-label experimentation. This pattern—already visible in psychedelics and nootropics—is now extending to mainstream pharmaceuticals, suggesting growing distrust of medical gatekeeping.

**Societal layer:** Control / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Functional beverages addressing hormonal health (cycle support, menopause symptoms) have significant opportunity, but must navigate regulatory constraints carefully. The peer-to-peer knowledge sharing model suggests community-based marketing may be more effective than traditional advertising.

**What to monitor:** Growth of women's health communities sharing pharmaceutical hacks; regulatory response to off-label promotion; consumer interest in hormone-supporting functional ingredients; emergence of cycle-syncing beverage products.

Consider supporting scenes without demanding visibility in return. The most valuable cultural capital may be invisible.

**What to monitor:** Emergence of location-protected subcultural spaces; growth of invite-only events and communities; consumer rejection of brands that "discover" subcultures; development of anti-documentation norms at events.

### SIGNAL 9: Queer Documentation as Aesthetic Category

**What happened:** Photographer Jesse Glazzard's monograph "The Sound of Dirt and Beauty" presents "dirty" images as beautiful, documenting queer life in West Yorkshire over a decade. The work explicitly rejects the clean, aspirational aesthetic that dominates commercial photography.

**Why it is a signal:** The equation of "dirt" with "beauty" represents a rejection of sanitized representation. This aesthetic—raw, unpolished, authentic—is gaining cultural currency as consumers tire of the filtered, optimized imagery that dominates social media and advertising.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Rebellion / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Visual marketing that embraces imperfection, mess, and authenticity may resonate more than polished production. Consider photography that shows real consumption moments—spills, condensation, imperfect pours—rather than idealized studio shots.

**What to monitor:** Growth of "anti-aesthetic" visual trends; consumer response to unpolished brand imagery; emergence of photographers and directors working in deliberately raw styles; rejection of AI-generated or heavily edited imagery.

### SIGNAL 10: Climate Disruption of Industrial Systems

**What happened:** A jellyfish swarm shut down three reactors at France's Gravelines nuclear power plant for the second consecutive year. Despite installing cameras and deploying fishing boats after last year's incident, the swarm overwhelmed defenses. Officials spent "hundreds of thousands of euros" on prevention that failed.

**Why it is a signal:** Climate change is creating cascading system failures that expensive technological solutions cannot prevent. This pattern—invest heavily in prevention, fail anyway—will become increasingly common across industries, including food and beverage supply chains.

**Societal layer:** Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Supply chain resilience must account for climate-driven disruptions that cannot be predicted or prevented. Diversification of ingredient sourcing, local production capabilities, and transparent communication about disruptions will become competitive advantages.

**What to monitor:** Climate-driven supply chain disruptions in beverage ingredients; consumer response to transparency about sourcing challenges; growth of local and regional production; insurance and risk management innovations for climate disruption.

Consider how flavors, textures, environments, and service styles accommodate different sensory needs. Avoid overstimulating environments that exclude neurodivergent consumers.

**What to monitor:** Growth of neurodivergent identity communities; consumer demand for sensory-friendly experiences; emergence of neurodivergent-focused product design; shifts in how brands communicate about cognitive difference.

### SIGNAL 12: NA Spirits Achieving Cocktail Parity

**What happened:** A Guardian cocktail recipe explicitly notes that a strawberry basil margarita "works very well with non-alcoholic tequila"—presented as a casual aside rather than a special accommodation. The NA option is mentioned alongside suggestions for repurposing the syrup.

**Why it is a signal:** When mainstream food media treats NA spirits as unremarkable substitutions rather than novelties requiring explanation, it signals normalization. The casual mention suggests NA options have achieved sufficient quality that they no longer require justification or apology.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The "NA as accommodation" framing is giving way to "NA as option." Marketing should reflect this shift—less emphasis on what's missing, more emphasis on what's present. Recipe development and bartender education should treat NA spirits as standard ingredients.

**What to monitor:** Frequency of NA mentions in mainstream food media; bartender attitudes toward NA spirits; consumer willingness to order NA cocktails in social settings; growth of NA-forward cocktail menus.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Invisible Menu

**Hypothesis:** Consumers seeking selective presence will respond positively to beverage experiences that reward intentional discovery over algorithmic visibility.

**Method:** Partner with 3-5 independent venues to create "invisible menu" items—NA cocktails or functional beverages available only to those who ask for them by name. Seed awareness through private community channels (Discord servers, WhatsApp groups, newsletter-only announcements) rather than public social media. Track how information spreads organically and whether the exclusivity increases perceived value.

**Success metric:** 40% of invisible menu orders come from customers who learned about items through peer recommendation rather than direct brand communication; average order value for invisible menu items exceeds standard menu items by 25%; social media mentions remain below threshold that would indicate mainstream discovery.

---

### Experiment 2: Earthwork Beverage Rituals

**Hypothesis:** Consumers hungry for nature-immersed, infrastructure-free experiences will engage deeply with beverage rituals that eliminate traditional service elements.

**Method:** Design a portable "earthwork beverage kit" for outdoor consumption—drinks served in biodegradable vessels or directly from natural containers (hollowed gourds, bamboo segments), accompanied by instructions for creating a communal drinking ritual without tables or chairs. Partner with retreat centers, outdoor experience companies, or land art practitioners to test in controlled settings. Document participant responses to the removal of familiar hospitality infrastructure.

**Success metric:** Net Promoter Score exceeds 70 among participants; 60% of participants report the experience as "meaningfully different" from standard beverage consumption; social sharing focuses on the ritual/experience rather than the product itself.

---

### Experiment 3: Cycle-Syncing Functional Beverage Subscription

**Hypothesis:** Women seeking solutions for hormonal health outside traditional medical channels will adopt functional beverages positioned as cycle-support tools, especially if distributed through peer-to-peer recommendation.

**Method:** Develop a 28-day functional beverage subscription with formulations varying across menstrual cycle phases (follicular, ovulatory, luteal, menstrual). Partner with women's health communities and influencers who already discuss cycle-syncing. Use community-based distribution (group orders, shared subscriptions) rather than individual e-commerce. Collect detailed feedback on symptom management and experience.

**Success metric:** 50% of subscribers acquired through peer recommendation rather than paid advertising; 65% report perceived improvement in at least one cycle-related symptom; subscription retention exceeds 60% after three cycles.
