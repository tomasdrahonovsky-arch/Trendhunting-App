## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound tension between authenticity-seeking and economic pragmatism, with consumers and industries alike navigating between genuine meaning-making and survival-mode adaptation. The hospitality sector is mobilising collectively around VAT reform (#VATsTheProblem reaching 100,000 signatures in 72 hours), signalling an industry at breaking point that's finally finding unified voice. Meanwhile, the drinks industry is fragmenting along fascinating lines: ancient provenance (Ötzi yeast brewing), functional economics (Garda DOC's 9% ABV wines exploiting UK duty structures), and consciousness alternatives (THC beverages assembling serious industry talent).

The cultural moment is defined by what we might call "strategic authenticity" — consumers want genuine experiences and provenance stories, but economic realities are reshaping what gets made and how it's positioned. The Garda DOC story is emblematic: a 9% ABV wine isn't primarily about wellness trends, it's about hitting price thresholds in a duty-graduated market. Yet it will be marketed as mindful drinking. This gap between commercial reality and consumer narrative creates both opportunity and risk.

In hospitality, we're seeing experience design become increasingly theatrical and exclusive — from Da Terra's 19kg nano-lot coffee to Seed Library NYC's ingredient-forward cocktail evolution. The message: in a compressed market, differentiation comes through story, scarcity, and sensory precision.

For non-alcoholic beverages, the implications are clear. The category must move beyond "alcohol alternative" positioning toward genuine cultural relevance — whether through provenance narratives, functional benefits that justify premium pricing, or integration into the emerging "fricy" (fruity-spicy) flavour movement gaining traction from South American influences. The winners will be those who understand that today's consumer wants both meaning and value, and refuses to accept that these are mutually exclusive.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Provenance Arms Race

**What happened:** Scientists plan to brew beer from 5,300-year-old yeast extracted from Ötzi the Iceman's remains, having already successfully baked sourdough from the same strains.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the extreme end of provenance storytelling — where origin narratives become so compelling they border on the absurd. It reflects consumer hunger for authenticity that transcends typical terroir claims, seeking connection to deep human history.

**Societal layer:** Identity / spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should explore heritage ingredients and historical brewing/fermentation traditions. The story of "how this was made before alcohol became the default" could be powerful — ancient tisanes, fermented tonics, botanical preparations with documented lineage.

**What to monitor:** Academic partnerships with beverage brands; consumer response to "archaeological" food/drink products; growth in heritage grain and heirloom ingredient sourcing.

---

### SIGNAL 2: Duty-Driven Product Design

**What happened:** Garda DOC wines at 9% ABV are positioned as strategically advantageous for UK retail, where graduated duty rates mean lower ABV translates to significantly lower shelf prices — a 9% wine pays £2.07 duty versus £3.10 for 13.5% ABV.

**Why it is a signal:** Product development is increasingly reverse-engineered from tax policy rather than consumer preference. This creates a new category of "economically optimised" beverages that happen to align with wellness narratives.

**Societal layer:** Control / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages already benefit from favourable duty treatment. The opportunity is to position this economic advantage as consumer benefit (better value) while layering wellness messaging on top. Brands should audit their pricing architecture against duty thresholds.

**What to monitor:** UK duty rate changes; retailer price-point strategies; emergence of "session strength" as a mainstream category descriptor.

### SIGNAL 3: Hospitality's Collective Voice Emerges

**What happened:** Tom Kerridge's #VATsTheProblem campaign gathered 100,000+ signatures in 72 hours, with unprecedented cross-industry support from UK Hospitality, BBPA, BII, and CODE Hospitality.

**Why it is a signal:** An industry historically fragmented by competition is unifying around survival. This collective action suggests hospitality operators recognise that individual brand-building is insufficient without systemic change.

**Societal layer:** Community / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should consider how they support hospitality venues during this pressure period. Partnerships that help venues improve margins (better NA drink margins than alcohol) could build lasting loyalty. Position NA as part of the solution, not another challenge.

**What to monitor:** Government response to VAT campaign; venue closure rates; hospitality operator sentiment surveys; emergence of other collective industry actions.

### SIGNAL 4: THC Beverages Professionalise

**What happened:** THC drinks company Reframe Beverage assembled an advisory board featuring veterans from Diageo, Moët Hennessy, Brown-Forman, and Southern Glazer's, signalling serious institutional investment in the category.

**Why it is a signal:** Cannabis beverages are transitioning from counterculture novelty to professionally managed CPG category. The talent migration from traditional alcohol suggests industry insiders see THC as a legitimate growth vector.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands face a more sophisticated competitor for the "alternative to alcohol" occasion. Differentiation must sharpen — NA offers social acceptability, workplace compatibility, and health positioning that THC cannot. Consider how to own the "clear-headed socialising" space before THC beverages claim it.

**What to monitor:** THC beverage distribution expansion; regulatory changes in key markets; consumer crossover between NA and THC occasions; talent movement between categories.

### SIGNAL 5: Micro-Scarcity as Experience Currency

**What happened:** Da Terra restaurant secured a 19kg nano-lot coffee (the smallest and highest-scoring from Daterra Estate's Masterpiece Auction) for exclusive service, while Seed Library NYC launched cocktails featuring hyper-specific, underutilised ingredients.

**Why it is a signal:** In compressed hospitality markets, differentiation increasingly comes through controlled scarcity and ingredient storytelling. The experience economy is evolving toward "unrepeatable moments" that justify premium pricing.

**Societal layer:** Status / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should develop limited-edition, story-rich releases that give venues exclusive offerings. Consider "vintage" NA releases, single-origin botanical batches, or chef/bartender collaborations that create FOMO-driven demand.

**What to monitor:** Fine dining NA beverage programmes; auction prices for rare ingredients; social media engagement on scarcity-positioned products; emergence of NA "allocated" products.

### SIGNAL 6: The Fricy Flavour Wave

**What happened:** South American-inspired "fricy" (fruity + spicy) drinks and dishes are gaining mainstream traction in the UK, with venues like Mango Twist in London serving chamoyada-style offerings to enthusiastic response.

**Why it is a signal:** Western palates are embracing more complex, challenging flavour profiles that combine sweetness with heat. This represents a maturation beyond "swicy" (sweet-spicy) toward more nuanced sensory experiences rooted in specific cultural traditions.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages should explore chilli-fruit combinations, tamarind, chamoy, and other Latin American flavour profiles. The category has been dominated by European botanical traditions; diversifying flavour heritage could unlock new consumer segments and occasions.

**What to monitor:** Mexican and South American ingredient imports; TikTok food trends featuring chilli-fruit combinations; menu adoption at mainstream casual dining; Gen Z flavour preference research.

### SIGNAL 7: Sparkling Water's Premiumisation

**What happened:** Flavoured sparkling waters (Dash, Aqua Libra, Yew) are being positioned as "fun drinks" that bring excitement to hydration, with chefs and founders framing them as lifestyle products rather than functional beverages.

**Why it is a signal:** The boundary between "water" and "beverage" is blurring. Sparkling water is being repositioned from neutral hydration to active choice with personality and occasion-appropriateness.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** This creates both competition and opportunity. NA brands should consider where they sit on the complexity spectrum — are they competing with premium sparkling water or offering something categorically different? Clear positioning against this rising middle-ground is essential.

**What to monitor:** Sparkling water brand proliferation; on-premise sparkling water programmes; consumer willingness to pay premium for flavoured water; category blur in retail shelving.

### SIGNAL 8: The Spirits Industry's Contraction

**What happened:** Brown-Forman reported flat sales outlook and impairment charges on Gin Mare and Diplomático acquisitions, citing "macroeconomic uncertainty" and declining whiskey demand.

**Why it is a signal:** Major spirits companies are struggling with premiumisation strategies that worked in the 2010s. Consumer discretionary spending on alcohol is compressing, creating potential white space for value-positioned alternatives.

**Societal layer:** Control / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** As premium spirits falter, NA brands have an opportunity to capture "trading down" consumers who still want sophisticated drinking experiences. Position NA as smart spending, not deprivation. The "affordable luxury" positioning becomes more relevant.

**What to monitor:** Spirits category volume trends; premium spirits pricing elasticity; consumer research on alcohol spending intentions; NA brand performance during economic uncertainty.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: Heritage Fermentation Series

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will pay a 40%+ premium for NA beverages with documented historical provenance and traditional fermentation methods, particularly when the story connects to pre-industrial drinking cultures.

**Method:** Develop a limited-edition NA fermented beverage using heritage grains or botanicals with documented historical use (e.g., medieval gruit herbs, ancient grain kvass, historical shrub recipes). Partner with a food historian or museum for authentication. Release through fine dining venues first, then direct-to-consumer. Price at £12-15 per 250ml bottle. Track purchase intent, repeat purchase, and social sharing of provenance story.

**Success metric:** 25% of purchasers share provenance story on social media; 40% repeat purchase rate; successful placement in 10+ fine dining venues within 90 days.

---

### Experiment 2: Fricy NA Spritz

**Hypothesis:** A chilli-fruit NA spritz positioned around the emerging "fricy" flavour trend will outperform traditional botanical NA options among 21-35 year olds in urban markets.

**Method:** Develop a chamoy-inspired NA spritz featuring mango, lime, and guajillo chilli. Partner with 3-5 Mexican or Latin American restaurants in London for exclusive launch. Create TikTok-native content showing preparation and cultural context. Conduct A/B testing against a traditional botanical spritz in the same venues. Measure velocity, reorder rates, and social engagement.

**Success metric:** 30% higher velocity than control product; 50% of social content generated by consumers (not brand); successful expansion to 15+ venues within 60 days.

---

### Experiment 3: Hospitality Margin Partnership Programme

**Hypothesis:** Venues under economic pressure will prioritise NA brands that demonstrably improve their margin profile, creating loyalty that outlasts the current crisis.

**Method:** Develop a "Margin Partner" programme offering venues: (1) NA products with 65%+ gross margin potential, (2) staff training on upselling NA options, (3) co-branded marketing materials positioning NA as "smart spending" for consumers. Recruit 20 venues facing margin pressure (identified through industry contacts). Track NA sales as percentage of total beverage revenue, venue profitability change, and brand loyalty metrics over 6 months.

**Success metric:** Participating venues increase NA beverage share from typical 3-5% to 10%+; 80% of venues continue partnership after trial period; programme generates positive trade press coverage.
