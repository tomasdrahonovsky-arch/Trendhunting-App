## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound recalibration of how people relate to consumption, community, and control. The most significant signal comes from South Korea, where nearly half of all neighbourhood pubs have vanished in eight years—not merely an economic story, but a generational rejection of mandatory social drinking rituals that once defined corporate culture. This mirrors broader global patterns: younger cohorts are drinking less, seeking more intentional social experiences, and demanding environments that serve their wellbeing rather than inherited norms.

Simultaneously, we observe a tension between curation and chaos. British diners overwhelmingly support adults-only restaurant options (76%, including 74% of parents), signalling a desire for controlled, predictable social atmospheres. Yet this sits alongside the raw, unfiltered energy of Nepal's largest-ever queer rave and Paris's Fête de la Musique street celebrations—suggesting people want both sanctuary and release, but on their own terms.

The hospitality industry faces a fundamental question: what is a venue for? The Korean pub closures suggest that spaces built primarily around alcohol consumption are structurally vulnerable when drinking becomes optional. Meanwhile, the James Halliday interview offers a counterpoint—wine as a vehicle for connection, memory, and shared experience rather than mere intoxication. "Great wines are meant to be shared," he says, articulating what the non-alcoholic beverage space must understand: the product is secondary to the ritual it enables.

Climate stress adds urgency. With extreme heat warnings across Europe and one in six English babies living in overheated homes, the physical conditions of gathering are changing. Hospitality must adapt not just to shifting values but to shifting temperatures. The brands that thrive will be those that understand they're selling belonging, not beverages.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Korean Pub Collapse

**What happened:** South Korea lost 46% of its neighbourhood pubs since 2018, with closures accelerating to eight per day in the past year. Domestic liquor shipments fell 17.3% over a decade, with the sharpest declines among younger adults.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the structural unwinding of drinking as social obligation. The decline of "hoesik" (mandatory after-work drinking) reflects younger workers rejecting inherited corporate rituals and prioritising health, autonomy, and alternative forms of connection.

**Societal layer:** Control / rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Markets with strong workplace drinking cultures (Japan, UK, Australia) may follow similar trajectories. Non-alcoholic options positioned as enabling professional socialising without compromise could capture significant share. The opportunity is not replacing alcohol but replacing the obligation.

**What to monitor:** Corporate wellness policies in Asia-Pacific; after-work socialising patterns among Gen Z professionals; emergence of "sober networking" events in major business centres.

---

### SIGNAL 2: The Sanctuary Economy

**What happened:** 76% of British diners support adults-only restaurant options, with Southampton (83%) and Bristol (83%) leading. Notably, 74% of parents also support the concept.

**Why it is a signal:** This isn't anti-child sentiment—it's pro-predictability. After years of ambient chaos (pandemic, political instability, economic precarity), consumers are willing to pay premium prices for guaranteed atmosphere. The desire for curated, controlled social environments is intensifying.

**Societal layer:** Control / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Premium non-alcoholic venues could position themselves as "sanctuary spaces"—adults-only, atmosphere-controlled, experience-guaranteed. The value proposition shifts from what you're drinking to what you're escaping.

**What to monitor:** Growth of "experience guarantee" policies in hospitality; noise-level marketing; emergence of atmosphere-as-amenity pricing.

---

### SIGNAL 3: The Pet Polarisation

**What happened:** While adults-only dining enjoys broad consensus, pet-friendly dining divides Britain sharply—63% support in Liverpool versus 25% in Belfast. Cities with high adults-only support often show low pet support.

**Why it is a signal:** This reveals that "inclusive" means different things to different communities. The hospitality industry cannot assume universal preferences; hyper-local positioning becomes essential. What works in one postcode may fail in another.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands must resist national campaigns in favour of city-specific or neighbourhood-specific positioning. A non-alcoholic bar concept that succeeds in Newcastle may need fundamental repositioning for Belfast.

**What to monitor:** Regional variation in lifestyle preferences; local social media sentiment analysis; emergence of "community fit" as a venue selection criterion.

---

### SIGNAL 4: Wine as Memory Technology

**What happened:** James Halliday, at 87, published his final book documenting 140 wine dinners over 50 years, emphasising that "great wines are meant to be shared" and that the joy lies in "hoping they see what you see in the glass."

**Why it is a signal:** This articulates wine's true value proposition: not intoxication but shared perception, memory creation, and intergenerational connection. The product is a vehicle for experience, not the experience itself.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic beverages must develop their own "memory technology"—rituals, language, and occasions that create shareable moments. The goal is not mimicking wine but achieving the same function: giving people something to see together.

**What to monitor:** Development of non-alcoholic tasting vocabularies; emergence of NA "collectors" and "vintages"; growth of NA pairing menus at fine dining establishments.

---

### SIGNAL 5: The Quarter-Life Recalibration

**What happened:** Dazed published a guide to surviving the "quarter-life crisis," describing a generation feeling "antsy," "floating through jelly," and questioning work, relationships, and location simultaneously.

**Why it is a signal:** Mid-20s identity instability is being normalised and even celebrated as "a period of growth." This cohort is actively seeking tools, rituals, and products that help them navigate uncertainty rather than escape it.

**Societal layer:** Identity / mental health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Products positioned as "clarity aids" rather than "escape mechanisms" may resonate strongly with this demographic. Marketing that acknowledges uncertainty rather than promising resolution could build authentic connection.

**What to monitor:** Growth of "intentional living" content among 23-28 year olds; emergence of "clarity" as a product benefit; decline of escapist messaging in beverage marketing.

---

### SIGNAL 6: Heat as Hospitality Constraint

**What happened:** Europe faces extreme heat warnings, with Paris experiencing dangerous temperatures during fashion week. One in six English babies live in overheated homes. Red heat alerts—signalling threat to life—have been issued.

**Why it is a signal:** Climate is becoming a primary constraint on when, where, and how people gather. Daytime socialising may become untenable in summer months; evening and night economies may expand; cooling becomes a hospitality amenity.

**Societal layer:** Control / community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Hydration-forward, cooling-positioned beverages gain functional relevance. Venues with effective climate control become destinations. "Cool spaces" may become as valuable as "third places."

**What to monitor:** Shift in peak hospitality hours during heat events; emergence of "cooling stations" as venue features; hydration messaging in beverage marketing.

### SIGNAL 7: The Queer Rave as Political Infrastructure

**What happened:** Nepal hosted its largest-ever queer rave in Kathmandu, prompting UK drag queen Bimini to note "this is what Pride should be about—a reminder of what we're fighting for."

**Why it is a signal:** As Pride becomes commercialised in Western markets, authentic queer celebration is migrating to unexpected geographies. The political function of gathering—not just the social function—is being reasserted.

**Societal layer:** Rebellion / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands seeking authentic Pride positioning should look beyond traditional markets. Supporting emerging queer spaces globally may offer more credible engagement than sponsoring established Western Pride events.

**What to monitor:** Growth of queer nightlife in non-Western markets; backlash against corporate Pride sponsorship; emergence of "political gathering" as distinct from "social gathering."

---

### SIGNAL 8: The Acne Influencer Paradox

**What happened:** Content creators who built audiences on acne-prone skin face existential crises when their skin clears—their identity, income, and community were built on a condition they sought to cure.

**Why it is a signal:** This reveals the dangerous entanglement of identity, condition, and commerce in creator economies. Authenticity becomes a trap when the authentic self changes.

**Societal layer:** Identity / status

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Brands building on "sober curious" or "wellness journey" narratives should consider what happens when the journey ends. Identity-based marketing may need evolution pathways built in.

**What to monitor:** Creator pivots when founding conditions change; emergence of "post-journey" content categories; brand relationships with creators whose circumstances evolve.

---

### SIGNAL 9: The England Flag Anxiety

**What happened:** Dazed explored the "ugly politics" of flying the England flag, noting that St George's Cross displays trigger anxiety about whether the intent is football support or far-right signalling.

**Why it is a signal:** National symbols have become semiotically unstable—their meaning depends entirely on context, and that context is increasingly contested. Brands using national imagery face interpretation risks.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Patriotic or national positioning carries significant risk in polarised environments. Local or regional identity may offer safer ground than national identity for brand positioning.

**What to monitor:** Semiotic stability of national symbols; regional identity as alternative to national identity; brand backlash incidents related to national imagery.

---

### SIGNAL 10: The Digital Literacy Imperative

**What happened:** UK Scouts are introducing badges in content creation, digital communication, and online safety after consulting 3,000 teenagers who said they wanted skills to navigate AI and social media.

**Why it is a signal:** Young people are explicitly requesting tools to manage digital environments they find overwhelming. Digital literacy is becoming a survival skill, not an optional enhancement.

**Societal layer:** Control / mental health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues and brands that offer "digital detox" or "low-stimulation" environments may find receptive audiences among digitally fatigued young people. The absence of digital engagement becomes a feature, not a bug.

**What to monitor:** Growth of "offline" as a lifestyle category; digital detox venue concepts; young consumer preferences for low-tech social environments.

---

### SIGNAL 11: The Physical Media Backlash

**What happened:** Rockstar Games announced GTA 6's "physical edition" will contain a download code rather than a disc, triggering significant consumer backlash despite the game's unprecedented anticipation.

**Why it is a signal:** Even in gaming—the most digitally native entertainment category—consumers are pushing back against the elimination of physical ownership. Tangibility retains value even when functionality is identical.

**Societal layer:** Control / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Physical, tangible elements of beverage experiences (bottles, labels, packaging) may carry more weight than assumed. The trend toward digital menus and QR codes may face similar resistance.

**What to monitor:** Consumer preference for physical menus; collectibility of beverage packaging; resistance to fully digital hospitality experiences.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Clarity Menu

**Hypothesis:** Mid-20s consumers experiencing "quarter-life crisis" will respond more positively to non-alcoholic beverages positioned as "clarity aids" (supporting clear thinking, intentional presence) than those positioned as "alcohol alternatives" or "health drinks."

**Method:** Partner with three venues in high-density young professional areas. Create two identical NA cocktail menus with different framing: one using traditional "mocktail" language, one using "clarity" language (e.g., "The Clear Head," "Present Tense," "Focused"). Track order rates, repeat purchases, and conduct exit interviews on purchase motivation over 8 weeks.

**Success metric:** 25% higher order rate for clarity-positioned menu; qualitative feedback indicating emotional resonance with positioning.

---

### Experiment 2: The Sanctuary Space

**Hypothesis:** Adults-only, atmosphere-guaranteed non-alcoholic bar concepts will command 20%+ price premiums over standard venues, with higher customer satisfaction scores.

**Method:** Create a pop-up "sanctuary bar" in Southampton or Bristol (highest adults-only support). Enforce adults-only policy, guarantee maximum decibel levels, and offer premium NA cocktails at 20% above market rate. Measure willingness to pay, dwell time, return intent, and Net Promoter Score against control venues.

**Success metric:** Maintained or increased traffic despite premium pricing; NPS 15+ points above comparable venues; 40%+ stated return intent.

---

### Experiment 3: The Memory Ritual

**Hypothesis:** Non-alcoholic beverages served with structured "memory rituals" (guided tasting notes, shared observation prompts, commemorative elements) will generate higher perceived value and social sharing than identical beverages served conventionally.

**Method:** Develop a "Memory Service" protocol for a premium NA beverage: includes tasting cards with guided questions ("What does this remind you of?"), a shared observation moment, and a small commemorative card. Test against standard service of the same beverage across 200 covers. Measure willingness to pay, social media sharing, and qualitative feedback on experience memorability.

**Success metric:** 30% higher willingness to pay for Memory Service; 3x social sharing rate; 50%+ of customers describing experience as "memorable" in follow-up survey.
