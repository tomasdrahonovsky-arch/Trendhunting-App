## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a hospitality sector riding the crest of communal sporting euphoria while simultaneously grappling with deeper questions about authenticity, accessibility, and the democratisation of taste. England's World Cup success has demonstrated that pubs remain irreplaceable social infrastructure—transactions surging 746% in the early hours post-victory—yet this celebration of collective experience sits alongside signals of cultural gatekeeping being dismantled across multiple fronts.

The Michelin Guide's expansion into wine ratings represents a significant power shift: the world's most recognised arbiter of culinary excellence is now codifying wine quality in ways that could either democratise access or further entrench hierarchy. Meanwhile, David Beckham's viral "wine shaming" incident exposes persistent class anxieties around beverage choice, revealing that what we drink in public remains a loaded social performance.

Younger consumers are sending mixed messages. Gen Z's "romanticising hangovers" trend on TikTok represents a cheeky rebellion against optimisation culture—a deliberate embrace of messiness as authenticity. Yet this coexists with the organic wine movement's continued momentum and Côtes du Rhône's strategic pivot toward "chilled wine" positioning to capture spontaneous, lifestyle-driven consumption occasions.

The through-line connecting these signals is a tension between curation and casualness, between expertise and accessibility. Sauternes producers are exploring cocktails to revive a struggling category. English sparkling wine celebrates two decades of patient craft. Celebrity-backed rosé launches trade on aristocratic provenance while claiming "modern luxury."

For non-alcoholic beverage brands, the opportunity lies in this liminal space: offering products that feel both considered and effortless, that can anchor communal celebration without requiring connoisseurship, and that allow consumers to participate in cultural moments without the class anxiety that still shadows alcohol consumption.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Pub as Social Infrastructure

**What happened:** England's World Cup victory drove pub transactions up 367% between 1am-3am, matching typical Saturday night levels. Extended licensing hours translated directly into commercial success, with 41,000 additional purchases across the trading window.

**Why it is a signal:** This demonstrates that physical hospitality venues remain irreplaceable for collective emotional experiences. Digital alternatives cannot replicate the communal catharsis of shared triumph.

**Societal layer:** Community / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic options must be positioned as full participants in celebration moments, not abstention choices. Venues need NA offerings that feel festive, shareable, and worthy of the occasion—not afterthoughts.

**What to monitor:** Extended licensing applications for major cultural events; NA beverage sales velocity during sporting tournaments; social media sentiment around "sober celebration" experiences.

### SIGNAL 2: Romanticising the Hangover

**What happened:** Gen Z users on TikTok and Instagram are "romanticising" hangovers—flaunting dark circles and headaches as badges of a good time, broadcasting their "bad decisions" with aesthetic gloss.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a deliberate rejection of body optimisation culture and wellness perfectionism. Messiness is being reclaimed as authenticity; imperfection as proof of living fully.

**Societal layer:** Rebellion / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The "clean living" positioning of many NA brands may alienate this cohort. There's an opportunity for NA products that embrace hedonism, excess, and the aesthetic of indulgence without requiring actual alcohol consumption.

**What to monitor:** Evolution of "bed rotting" and "feral girl" aesthetics; NA brand engagement among accounts posting hangover content; emergence of "morning after" rituals that don't involve alcohol recovery.

### SIGNAL 3: Michelin's Wine Power Play

**What happened:** The Michelin Guide launched its first-ever wine ratings in Burgundy, awarding "Three MICHELIN Grapes" to nine estates including Domaine de la Romanée-Conti. The system mirrors their restaurant star methodology.

**Why it is a signal:** The world's most recognised hospitality authority is extending its credentialing power into wine, potentially reshaping how consumers navigate quality and how producers compete for recognition.

**Societal layer:** Status / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** This signals appetite for trusted curation in beverage categories. NA beverages lack equivalent credentialing systems—an opportunity for authoritative rating frameworks that help consumers navigate an increasingly crowded market.

**What to monitor:** Michelin's expansion into other wine regions and potentially spirits; emergence of competing rating systems; consumer response to "graded" wine purchasing.

### SIGNAL 4: Class Anxiety in Public Drinking

**What happened:** David Beckham was publicly shamed on social media for drinking red wine from a large glass at a World Cup match, with critics calling it "posh," "wankery," and highlighting the divide between VIP boxes and regular stands.

**Why it is a signal:** Beverage choice in public spaces remains a loaded class signifier. Wine consumption—particularly with "proper" glassware—triggers resentment about access, privilege, and performative sophistication.

**Societal layer:** Status / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages have an opportunity to sidestep class coding entirely, positioning as democratically accessible without the baggage of wine snobbery or beer tribalism. Glassware and presentation choices matter enormously.

**What to monitor:** Social media discourse around beverage choices at public events; evolution of "acceptable" drinks in different social contexts; NA brand positioning relative to class signifiers.

### SIGNAL 5: Chilling Wine to Chill Out

**What happened:** Côtes du Rhône launched a "Refresh Your Wine" campaign targeting 21-40 year olds, positioning chilled wines (including reds) as contemporary, spontaneous, and lifestyle-aligned rather than occasion-bound.

**Why it is a signal:** Traditional wine regions are abandoning formality to compete for casual consumption occasions. Temperature becomes a proxy for accessibility—chilled wine signals relaxation, not ritual.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Temperature and serving ritual are powerful positioning tools. NA beverages should consider how serving temperature communicates brand personality—cold signals casual and refreshing; room temperature or warm suggests contemplation and craft.

**What to monitor:** Wine serving temperature trends in hospitality; consumer language around "refreshing" vs. "serious" beverages; seasonal consumption pattern shifts.

### SIGNAL 6: Cocktails as Category Rescue

**What happened:** Sauternes producer Château d'Arche is actively pursuing mixology partnerships to revive the struggling sweet wine category, noting that "the demand for Sauternes in cocktails comes from final customers and the new wave of mixologists."

**Why it is a signal:** Traditional categories facing decline are turning to cocktail culture as a gateway for new consumers. Mixology becomes a translation layer between heritage products and contemporary drinking occasions.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Rebellion

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA spirits and complex botanicals should prioritise mixologist relationships and cocktail applications. The "neat pour" may be less important than versatility in mixed drinks for category growth.

**What to monitor:** Cocktail menu evolution in premium bars; mixologist influencer partnerships with heritage brands; consumer willingness to order unfamiliar ingredients in cocktail form.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Celebration Serve

**Hypothesis:** Non-alcoholic beverages positioned with festive presentation rituals (specific glassware, theatrical pours, shareable formats) will achieve higher sales velocity during major sporting events than standard serves.

**Method:** Partner with 10 pubs in England during remaining World Cup matches. Introduce a "Victory Serve" NA option: a premium botanical sparkling drink served in champagne-style glassware with a ceremonial pop, priced at parity with celebratory alcoholic options. Track sales against standard NA offerings and gather qualitative feedback on social acceptability.

**Success metric:** 40% higher NA sales velocity during match periods compared to control venues; positive social media mentions of the serve ritual; repeat purchase intent above 60%.

---

### Experiment 2: The Hangover Aesthetic

**Hypothesis:** An NA beverage brand that embraces "morning after" positioning—marketed as the drink for people who went hard last night (whether drinking alcohol or not)—will resonate with Gen Z consumers rejecting wellness perfectionism.

**Method:** Develop a limited-edition NA product with deliberately "rough" branding (muted colours, imperfect typography, copy acknowledging messiness). Seed with TikTok creators who post hangover content. Position as "the drink for when you're not trying to be your best self." Track engagement, sentiment, and purchase intent against traditional wellness-positioned NA competitors.

**Success metric:** 25% higher engagement rate than wellness-positioned NA content; positive sentiment ratio above 3:1; conversion to purchase among target demographic exceeding 15%.

---

### Experiment 3: The NA Sommelier Rating

**Hypothesis:** Introducing a credible, third-party rating system for non-alcoholic beverages (modelled on Michelin's wine grapes) will increase consumer confidence and willingness to pay premium prices.

**Method:** Convene a panel of respected beverage professionals (sommeliers, bartenders, food writers) to blind-taste and rate 50 NA beverages across categories. Publish ratings with detailed tasting notes through a dedicated platform. Partner with three premium retailers to display ratings at point of sale. Measure impact on sales velocity and price elasticity for rated products.

**Success metric:** Rated products achieve 20% higher sales velocity than unrated equivalents; consumer surveys show 35% increase in purchase confidence when ratings are visible; media coverage establishes the rating system as authoritative reference.
