## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a drinks industry in profound structural transition, where legacy business models are collapsing while new value systems emerge around regeneration, moderation, and experience-seeking. The fine wine sector faces existential pressure—UK's top 15 fine wine companies are collectively losing £20 million annually, prompting calls for radical efficiency. Meanwhile, major players like Endeavour Group are writing off AU$311 million in assets, signalling that vertical integration in alcohol is no longer viable.

Yet within this contraction, strategic clarity is emerging. Heineken's regenerative barley programme represents a new template: brands paying premiums directly to farmers for sustainable practices, creating differentiated supply chains that resonate with values-driven consumers. Bordeaux's revival of "claret" as a chillable, low-tannin category demonstrates legacy regions adapting to consumption patterns favouring cold-served, sessionable drinks.

The moderation narrative continues to mature beyond abstinence. New research confirms Gen Z engagement with alcohol is actually increasing, but through a lens of "empowerment of self-moderation"—consumers paying closer attention to alcohol content and choosing occasions that align with personal goals. This isn't rejection; it's curation. The semaglutide trial showing reduced heavy drinking days hints at pharmaceutical interventions entering the moderation conversation, potentially reshaping how we think about consumption control.

Regulatory volatility remains a wildcard. Australia's 79th alcohol tax hike in 40 years is driving demand for illicit alternatives, while Minnesota's THC drinks industry received a temporary reprieve from federal restrictions. The message: alternative beverage categories exist in regulatory limbo, creating both risk and opportunity.

For non-alcoholic beverage builders, the strategic imperative is clear: build brands that offer the social connection consumers seek (35% cite this as primary drinking motivation) while delivering on premium affordability and values alignment. The winners will be those who understand that today's consumer isn't choosing between drinking and not drinking—they're choosing how to belong.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: Fine Wine's Structural Collapse Forces Efficiency Revolution

**What happened:** Analysis reveals UK's top 15 fine wine companies are losing nearly £20 million annually, with £210 million spent on admin expenses to sell £850 million in wine. Industry leaders warn that "holding out for the market to bounce back" represents denial, and consolidation is inevitable.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the end of the collector-driven fine wine model. The observation that millennials and Gen Z won't build "3,000-4,000 case wine collections" signals a generational shift from accumulation to experience.

**Societal layer:** Status / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Premium NA brands should avoid replicating fine wine's inventory-heavy, collector-focused model. Instead, build for velocity and experience—limited releases consumed immediately rather than cellared. Position NA as the "anti-collection" category.

**What to monitor:** Fine wine merchant closures; emergence of wine-as-a-service models; shifts in auction house strategies toward younger demographics.

---

### SIGNAL 2: Regenerative Agriculture Becomes Brand Differentiator

**What happened:** Heineken launched a regenerative malting barley programme paying farmers direct premiums, aiming for nearly half of UK-sourced barley to be regeneratively grown by 2027. The initiative spans their entire portfolio including Heineken, Birra Moretti, and Foster's.

**Why it is a signal:** This moves sustainability from marketing claim to supply chain architecture. By making regenerative practices "commercially viable" for farmers, Heineken is building defensible differentiation that competitors cannot quickly replicate.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Spirituality

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should establish direct relationships with regenerative ingredient suppliers now, before premiums increase. Consider "farm-to-bottle" storytelling that names specific growers and practices—transparency as competitive advantage.

**What to monitor:** Regenerative certification standards; consumer willingness to pay premiums for regenerative claims; competitor supply chain announcements.

---

### SIGNAL 3: Gen Z Alcohol Engagement Increasing Through Moderation Lens

**What happened:** IWSR/Ipsos research commissioned by AB InBev reveals 76% of legal-drinking-age adults drink alcohol, with Gen Z engagement specifically increasing. However, consumers are "paying more attention to the alcohol content" and choosing options aligned with personal goals.

**Why it is a signal:** This contradicts the "Gen Z doesn't drink" narrative. The reality is more nuanced: they drink selectively, with intention. Moderation isn't abstinence—it's curation.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Position NA beverages not as alcohol replacements but as part of a moderation toolkit. Create "session strategies" that blend NA and low-ABV options. The opportunity is in the AND, not the OR.

**What to monitor:** ABV transparency labelling adoption; "drink counting" app usage; emergence of moderation-focused social occasions.

### SIGNAL 4: Bordeaux Reinvents "Claret" for Chillable Red Category

**What happened:** Bordeaux introduced a regulated "mention traditionnelle" called Bordeaux Claret for the 2025 vintage—light, low-tannin, unoaked reds designed to be served chilled. The specification requires short maceration (2-4 days) and targets younger consumers abandoning traditional reds.

**Why it is a signal:** A benchmark wine region is fundamentally reimagining its product for contemporary consumption patterns. This legitimises "chillable red" as a serious category, not a gimmick.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Develop NA red wine alternatives specifically designed for chilled service. The "refreshment" positioning that works for NA whites and rosés can now extend to reds. Consider temperature as a product design parameter.

**What to monitor:** Claret adoption rates in UK and Asian markets; emergence of chillable red categories from other regions; hospitality serving temperature innovations.

---

### SIGNAL 5: Pharmaceutical Moderation Enters Mainstream Conversation

**What happened:** A phase 2 clinical trial found oral semaglutide (Ozempic's active ingredient) significantly reduced heavy drinking days among people with alcohol use disorder, though it didn't meet primary craving endpoints. Researchers support larger trials.

**Why it is a signal:** The medicalisation of moderation is accelerating. If GLP-1 drugs become accepted tools for alcohol reduction, the "willpower" narrative around drinking less shifts to "optimisation."

**Societal layer:** Control / Mental Health

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Prepare for a consumer segment using pharmaceutical aids to reduce alcohol consumption. These consumers will still want sophisticated beverage experiences—they're not choosing sobriety, they're managing intake. Position NA as the premium choice for "optimised" drinkers.

**What to monitor:** GLP-1 prescription rates for alcohol use; emergence of "biohacker" drinking protocols; insurance coverage for alcohol reduction medications.

### SIGNAL 6: Tax Policy Driving Illicit Alcohol Growth

**What happened:** Australia's 79th alcohol tax hike in 40 years pushes spirits tax to AU$110.15 per litre—the third highest globally. Industry warns this is "driving demand for cheap, black-market alternatives," with illicit alcohol now comprising 7.5% of total Australian alcohol volume.

**Why it is a signal:** Punitive taxation is creating parallel markets. When legal products become unaffordable, consumers don't stop drinking—they find alternatives outside regulated channels.

**Societal layer:** Rebellion / Control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** In high-tax markets, NA beverages gain relative price competitiveness. Position premium NA as the "legal luxury"—sophisticated drinking without the tax burden. This is particularly relevant for on-premise where price sensitivity is acute.

**What to monitor:** Illicit alcohol market share by country; tax policy changes in key markets; consumer price sensitivity thresholds.

### SIGNAL 7: THC Beverages in Regulatory Limbo

**What happened:** Minnesota's THC drinks industry received a temporary reprieve from federal restrictions, with new regulations delayed until November. Senator Tina Smith called Minnesota "one of the strongest, most carefully regulated systems in the country" for hemp products.

**Why it is a signal:** THC beverages exist in a regulatory grey zone that could either legitimise or eliminate the category. Minnesota's model—careful regulation rather than prohibition—may become the template.

**Societal layer:** Rebellion / Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Monitor THC beverage regulations closely. If federal policy clarifies favourably, THC becomes a major NA competitor. If restrictions tighten, displaced THC consumers may seek sophisticated NA alternatives. Either outcome creates strategic opportunity.

**What to monitor:** Federal THC beverage policy; state-by-state regulatory approaches; craft brewer THC product launches.

### SIGNAL 8: Social Connection Remains Primary Drinking Motivation

**What happened:** IWSR research found 35% of consumers cite social occasions as their main motivation for drinking—the leading reason identified. The "experience economy" continues to drive consumption patterns.

**Why it is a signal:** Despite all the health and moderation discourse, drinking remains fundamentally social. Products that facilitate connection will outperform those positioned purely on functional benefits.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Community

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Design NA beverages for sharing occasions, not solo consumption. Consider formats that encourage social rituals—shareable bottles, cocktail-building kits, conversation-starting packaging. The product is a prop for connection.

**What to monitor:** Social occasion frequency trends; group dining vs. solo dining patterns; emergence of NA-specific social formats.

### SIGNAL 9: Heatwaves Accelerating Cold-Serve Category Growth

**What happened:** Analysis of European heatwave impacts reveals consumption shifting toward cold-served wines (sparkling, white, rosé, orange), with red wine's global share falling below 50% for the first time. Producers are adapting with blanc de noirs and chillable red innovations.

**Why it is a signal:** Climate change is reshaping beverage preferences in real-time. "Refreshment" is becoming a baseline expectation, not a seasonal consideration.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Ensure all NA products deliver on refreshment. Consider temperature-activated packaging, frozen formats, or "extra cold" serving recommendations. The NA category should own refreshment more aggressively than alcohol.

**What to monitor:** Regional temperature trends; cold-serve category growth rates; refrigeration/cooling innovations in hospitality.

### SIGNAL 10: Tradwife Content Spawning Next Generation

**What happened:** A 19-year-old married woman's TikTok about choosing traditional domesticity over college/career garnered 16 million views and 20,000+ comments, sparking widespread debate about young women embracing "tradwife" lifestyles.

**Why it is a signal:** Counter-cultural domesticity is gaining traction among Gen Z women, representing a rejection of girlboss narratives. This has implications for how brands speak to young female consumers about aspiration and identity.

**Societal layer:** Identity / Rebellion / Belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Avoid assumptions about what "empowerment" means to young female consumers. Some segments are embracing domesticity, homemaking, and traditional hospitality. Consider NA products positioned for home entertaining and domestic rituals rather than career-adjacent occasions.

**What to monitor:** Tradwife content engagement rates; home entertaining trend data; domestic ritual product categories.

### SIGNAL 11: Longevity Movement Facing Backlash

**What happened:** Bryan Johnson publicly questioned whether he's "taken this whole longevity thing too far," signalling potential fatigue with extreme biohacking protocols that include 100+ daily pills, restricted eating windows, and constant monitoring.

**Why it is a signal:** The optimisation-at-all-costs mentality may be peaking. Even its most visible proponent is expressing doubt, suggesting consumer appetite for extreme wellness protocols may be waning.

**Societal layer:** Control / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Position NA beverages as "sustainable wellness" rather than extreme optimisation. The message should be balance and enjoyment, not deprivation and monitoring. Consumers may be ready for permission to enjoy rather than optimise.

**What to monitor:** Biohacker influencer sentiment shifts; wellness fatigue indicators; "intuitive" health content growth.

### SIGNAL 12: Norfolk Brewery Emphasises Regional Identity

**What happened:** Woodforde's Brewery rebranded its keg range to celebrate Norfolk roots, including renaming products and incorporating local landmarks. The brewery explicitly positioned regional identity as a differentiator in a commoditised market.

**Why it is a signal:** In an era of global brands, hyper-local identity is becoming a competitive advantage. Provenance storytelling is shifting from "where it's made" to "what that place means."

**Societal layer:** Belonging / Identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands should consider deep regional positioning rather than generic "craft" claims. Specific place-based stories create authenticity that global brands cannot replicate. Consider partnerships with local landmarks, artists, or cultural institutions.

**What to monitor:** Regional brand performance vs. national brands; consumer research on provenance importance; local partnership models.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: The Moderation Toolkit Bundle

**Hypothesis:** Consumers practicing intentional moderation will purchase curated bundles combining NA beverages with low-ABV options, positioned as "session strategies" for social occasions, at a premium to individual products.

**Method:** Partner with a low-ABV wine or beer producer to create a limited "Session Kit" containing 2 NA beverages and 2 low-ABV drinks, with serving suggestions for a 4-hour social occasion. Include a simple guide explaining the "moderation math" (total units, pacing recommendations). Sell direct-to-consumer and through select independent bottle shops. Price at 15% premium to buying components separately.

**Success metric:** 500 kit sales in 8 weeks; post-purchase survey showing 70%+ would repurchase; social media shares of "session" occasions using the kit.

---

### Experiment 2: Chillable Red NA Wine Launch

**Hypothesis:** Following Bordeaux's Claret innovation, there is unmet demand for NA red wine specifically designed and marketed for chilled service, targeting consumers who have abandoned traditional reds for refreshment reasons.

**Method:** Develop or source an NA red wine with low tannins and fruit-forward profile. Package with prominent "Serve Chilled" messaging and temperature indicator on label. Launch with summer positioning and partner with 10 on-premise venues to serve exclusively from wine fridges alongside whites and rosés. Create content explaining the "chillable red" concept.

**Success metric:** On-premise reorder rate of 60%+ within 6 weeks; consumer taste test preference vs. room-temperature NA red; earned media coverage of "chillable red" trend.

---

### Experiment 3: Regenerative Ingredient Transparency Platform

**Hypothesis:** Consumers will engage more deeply with NA brands that provide transparent, traceable information about regenerative farming practices in their supply chain, and this engagement will correlate with purchase intent and willingness to pay premium prices.

**Method:** Create a simple digital platform (QR code on packaging) that tells the story of one specific regenerative farm supplying a key ingredient. Include farmer video, soil health metrics, and carbon impact data. A/B test packaging with and without QR code/regenerative messaging. Survey scanners on purchase intent and price sensitivity.

**Success metric:** 15%+ QR code scan rate; scanners show 25%+ higher purchase intent than non-scanners; 20%+ of scanners willing to pay 10%+ premium for regenerative-sourced product.
