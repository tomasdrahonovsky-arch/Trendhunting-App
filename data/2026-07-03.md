## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Today's cultural landscape reveals a profound recalibration of what "moderation" means across British and American society—not as deprivation, but as a sophisticated form of self-authorship. The emergence of "mid-strength" wines (6-8.5% ABV) with celebrity backing from Kylie Minogue and Dame Jessica Ennis-Hill signals that reduced alcohol is shedding its apologetic positioning. Ennis-Hill's framing—"something beautifully made, but lighter for modern life"—captures the zeitgeist: consumers want permission to participate fully in social rituals while maintaining agency over their bodies and mornings.

This dovetails with a broader hospitality reconfiguration. Andy Burnham's proposed 20% business rates cut for pubs signals political recognition that physical gathering spaces are cultural infrastructure, not merely commercial real estate. The tension between warehouse economics (Amazon) and high-street conviviality is being explicitly politicised—a rare moment where hospitality's social value is being quantified against digital convenience.

Meanwhile, the friction around reservation platforms (Resy, OpenTable, Tock) reveals consumer exhaustion with fragmented digital gatekeeping. People want to gather, but the infrastructure of access has become unnecessarily complex—creating opportunity for venues that simplify rather than complicate the path to presence.

The World Cup's arrival is catalysing massive activation spending (Budweiser's 2,095% volume spike at Bestway depots), demonstrating that sporting occasions remain unparalleled drivers of communal consumption. Yet the simultaneous rise of vinho verde appreciation—praised for its "gluggability" and moderate ABV—suggests that even within celebration, lighter options are gaining cultural legitimacy.

The through-line: consumers are seeking calibrated experiences that honour both pleasure and presence, rejecting the binary of abstinence versus excess.

## TREND SIGNALS

### SIGNAL 1: The Legitimisation of "Mid-Strength" Wine

**What happened:** UK supermarkets, led by Sainsbury's, have created dedicated retail bays for wines between 6-8.5% ABV. Celebrity-backed brands from Kylie Minogue (7% ABV, £8) and Dame Jessica Ennis-Hill (7% ABV, £12.50) are driving category growth, with Ennis-Hill explicitly positioning her product as "lighter for modern life."

**Why it is a signal:** This represents the normalisation of moderation within traditional alcohol categories—not as a compromise but as a premium choice. The celebrity endorsements from figures associated with discipline (Olympic athlete) and glamour (pop icon) reframe lower ABV as aspirational rather than restrictive.

**Societal layer:** Control / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Non-alcoholic brands face both opportunity and threat. The "mid-strength" category validates moderation-seeking behaviour, potentially expanding the addressable market. However, it also creates a "good enough" middle ground that may satisfy consumers who might otherwise have migrated to 0% options. NA brands must sharpen their positioning around complete clarity and morning-after benefits.

**What to monitor:** Retail shelf space allocation between mid-strength and 0% categories; whether mid-strength cannibalises NA growth or expands the overall moderation market; consumer language around "balance" versus "abstinence."

### SIGNAL 2: Political Valorisation of Physical Gathering Spaces

**What happened:** Andy Burnham, expected to become UK Prime Minister on 20 July, has pledged a 20% business rates cut for pubs, bars, and nightclubs, funded by increased levies on Amazon-style warehouses. This explicitly frames hospitality venues as socially valuable infrastructure deserving protection against digital commerce.

**Why it is a signal:** This marks a rare political acknowledgment that physical gathering spaces serve functions beyond commerce—they are sites of community formation, mental health support, and cultural identity. The framing of warehouses versus pubs as a zero-sum policy choice crystallises a broader societal debate about what kind of economy we want.

**Societal layer:** Community / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Hospitality venues may experience renewed investment and reduced closure rates, expanding the on-premise channel for NA beverages. Brands should prepare for a potential hospitality renaissance by strengthening on-trade relationships and developing products specifically designed for pub and bar occasions.

**What to monitor:** Policy implementation timelines; pub closure rates post-policy; whether other political parties adopt similar positions; consumer sentiment toward "supporting local" venues.

### SIGNAL 3: Reservation Platform Fatigue

**What happened:** Eater documented widespread consumer frustration with fragmented restaurant reservation systems—Resy, OpenTable, Tock, and direct booking all competing, with inconsistent functionality and poor discoverability. Users report that restaurants they know exist fail to appear in searches.

**Why it is a signal:** The infrastructure of access to hospitality experiences has become a friction point rather than an enabler. This suggests opportunity for venues that simplify booking or bypass platforms entirely, and indicates that the "discovery" phase of dining has become unnecessarily complex.

**Societal layer:** Control / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues that reduce booking friction may gain competitive advantage. For NA-focused establishments, this suggests value in direct relationship-building with customers (membership models, SMS booking, walk-in prioritisation) rather than platform dependence. The frustration also indicates appetite for curated, trusted recommendations over algorithmic discovery.

**What to monitor:** Platform consolidation moves; emergence of alternative booking models; consumer adoption of direct venue relationships; whether high-demand venues abandon platforms.

### SIGNAL 4: Sporting Events as Communal Consumption Catalysts

**What happened:** Budweiser's World Cup activation at Bestway depots drove volume increases of 2,095% at Park Royal, 197% in Manchester, and 60% in Liverpool. The activation combined exclusive deals, immersive experiences, and retailer support, demonstrating the commercial power of aligning with major sporting occasions.

**Why it is a signal:** Despite fragmentation of media and social experiences, major sporting events retain unparalleled power to synchronise collective behaviour and drive consumption spikes. The scale of these increases suggests that communal viewing occasions represent distinct consumption moments with different dynamics than everyday drinking.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA brands are significantly under-indexed in sporting occasion marketing. The communal, extended-duration nature of match viewing (90+ minutes) creates ideal conditions for session-friendly, lower-impact beverages. Brands should develop World Cup and major tournament activation strategies that position NA options as enabling longer, more present participation.

**What to monitor:** NA brand presence at sporting activations; consumer behaviour during extended viewing sessions; retailer willingness to stock NA options in sporting occasion displays.

### SIGNAL 5: Taste-Led Wine Navigation

**What happened:** Jacob's Creek relaunched in the UK with labels featuring taste profiles ("Juicy & Smooth," "Refreshing & Lively") rather than grape varieties, supported by a major "Welcome to the Creek" campaign. The strategy explicitly addresses consumer confusion around wine terminology.

**Why it is a signal:** This represents a category-wide acknowledgment that traditional wine language excludes rather than invites. The shift toward experiential descriptors over technical terminology suggests broader consumer preference for outcome-based product communication.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / pleasure

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages should adopt similar outcome-focused communication—describing how products make consumers feel rather than what they contain. Terms like "evening unwind," "social spark," or "morning clarity" may resonate more than ingredient lists or production methods.

**What to monitor:** Consumer response to taste-led labelling; whether competitors follow; application of similar approaches in NA categories; retail feedback on shoppability improvements.

### SIGNAL 6: Vinho Verde as Summer Archetype

**What happened:** The Guardian positioned Portuguese vinho verde as "the perfect summer wine," emphasising its low ABV (usually below 12%), high acidity, slight spritz, and "gluggability"—explicitly connecting moderate alcohol with refreshment and extended social occasions.

**Why it is a signal:** The cultural elevation of a traditionally modest wine category based on its sessionability and refreshment qualities indicates shifting criteria for what makes a drink "good." Quality is being redefined around drinkability and occasion-appropriateness rather than intensity or complexity.

**Societal layer:** Pleasure / control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beverages should study vinho verde's positioning success. The emphasis on spritz, acidity, and refreshment provides a template for NA product development and communication. Summer occasions represent a strategic battleground where NA options can compete directly on refreshment credentials.

**What to monitor:** Vinho verde sales growth; consumer language around "sessionability"; emergence of NA products explicitly positioned as summer/refreshment options; crossover between wine and NA consumer segments.

### SIGNAL 7: The Erosion of London's Club Kid Culture

**What happened:** Dazed documented the decline of London's fashion-forward club scene, attributing it to venue closures and the disappearance of spaces where experimental self-presentation could flourish. The piece frames nightlife as essential infrastructure for creative identity formation.

**Why it is a signal:** The loss of nocturnal spaces for identity experimentation has downstream effects on fashion, music, and broader cultural production. This signals both a crisis in creative infrastructure and potential demand for alternative spaces where similar functions can occur.

**Societal layer:** Identity / rebellion / belonging

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** The decline of traditional nightlife creates opportunity for alternative gathering formats—daytime parties, sober raves, creative workshops—that serve similar identity-formation functions without requiring late-night alcohol-centric venues. NA brands could sponsor or create such spaces, positioning themselves as enablers of creative community.

**What to monitor:** Emergence of alternative creative gathering formats; daytime party culture growth; sober rave attendance; creative community migration patterns.

### SIGNAL 8: Food Runners Overwhelmed by Delivery Demands

**What happened:** Eater reported that restaurant food runners are increasingly burdened by third-party delivery order assembly, fundamentally changing their job from table service to logistics coordination. The piece highlights tension between dine-in hospitality and delivery operations.

**Why it is a signal:** The operational strain of serving two distinct customer bases (in-venue and delivery) is creating service quality trade-offs. This suggests potential bifurcation between venues optimised for presence and those optimised for delivery—with implications for how beverages are experienced.

**Societal layer:** Community / control

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** Venues prioritising dine-in experience may become more attractive partners for NA brands seeking to build on-premise presence. The delivery channel, while growing, may be less suitable for beverages requiring context and presentation. Brands should identify and cultivate relationships with venues explicitly committed to in-person hospitality.

**What to monitor:** Venue decisions to limit or eliminate delivery; consumer preference for delivery-free establishments; service quality metrics at high-delivery-volume venues.

### SIGNAL 9: Digital ID for Alcohol Verification

**What happened:** The UK Home Office announced that certified Digital Verification Services will be accepted for alcohol sales from autumn 2026, modernising age verification beyond physical documents. Industry experts emphasised the need for staff training and consumer education.

**Why it is a signal:** Digital identity infrastructure is being built that will change the friction points around alcohol purchase. This creates both opportunities (smoother transactions) and challenges (potential for new forms of fraud or exclusion) for the beverage industry.

**Societal layer:** Control / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** While primarily affecting alcohol sales, digital ID normalisation may eventually enable more sophisticated personalisation and loyalty programmes across all beverage categories. NA brands should monitor how digital identity infrastructure evolves and consider how it might enable new customer relationship models.

**What to monitor:** Consumer adoption rates of digital ID; fraud incidents; integration with loyalty and personalisation systems; extension to non-age-restricted purchases.

### SIGNAL 10: Beer as American Identity Marker

**What happened:** A Beer Institute survey found that 64% of American adults consider beer "part of American tradition," with 55% selecting it as the drink that "feels most American"—surpassing soda, iced tea, and spirits. The finding coincides with America's 250th anniversary celebrations.

**Why it is a signal:** Beer's association with national identity creates both opportunity and constraint. It suggests that beverage choices carry symbolic weight beyond taste—they signal belonging to cultural communities. This has implications for how NA alternatives position themselves relative to traditional categories.

**Societal layer:** Belonging / identity

**Implication for non-alcoholic beverages / hospitality:** NA beer may benefit from beer's cultural centrality—offering a way to participate in "American tradition" without alcohol. However, NA brands in other categories (seltzers, functional beverages) may need to build their own cultural associations rather than borrowing from beer's heritage.

**What to monitor:** NA beer growth relative to other NA categories; cultural positioning of NA options in patriotic/traditional contexts; generational differences in beverage-identity associations.

## TOP 3 EXPERIMENTS

### Experiment 1: "Match Day Clarity" NA Activation Kit

**Hypothesis:** Sports viewing occasions represent an under-exploited opportunity for NA beverages, where extended duration (90+ minutes) and communal setting create ideal conditions for session-friendly, lower-impact drinks that enable sustained presence and participation.

**Method:** Partner with 10 independent pubs in World Cup host cities to create "Match Day Clarity" activations. Provide branded NA beverage stations, glassware designed for extended sessions, and point-of-sale materials emphasising "stay sharp for extra time." Track sales velocity, consumer feedback, and comparison with alcohol sales during same matches. Include post-match surveys on energy levels, enjoyment, and likelihood to repeat.

**Success metric:** NA beverage sales comprising >15% of total match-day beverage revenue; >70% of NA purchasers reporting they would choose NA again for future matches; measurable increase in average viewing duration among NA consumers.

---

### Experiment 2: Taste-Outcome Labelling A/B Test

**Hypothesis:** Consumers respond more positively to outcome-focused product descriptions ("evening unwind," "social spark") than ingredient-focused descriptions ("botanical blend," "adaptogenic formula"), mirroring the success of Jacob's Creek's taste-led wine repositioning.

**Method:** Create two versions of packaging and point-of-sale materials for an existing NA product line—one using current ingredient-focused language, one using outcome-focused language. Deploy in matched retail environments (similar demographics, footfall) for 8 weeks. Measure sales velocity, consumer dwell time at shelf, and conduct exit interviews on purchase drivers and product understanding.

**Success metric:** >20% sales lift for outcome-focused labelling; >30% improvement in consumer ability to articulate what the product "does for them"; qualitative feedback indicating reduced confusion and increased purchase confidence.

---

### Experiment 3: "Creative Assembly" Daytime Gathering Series

**Hypothesis:** The decline of nightlife spaces for creative identity formation creates demand for alternative gathering formats where similar functions (self-expression, community formation, creative experimentation) can occur without late-night, alcohol-centric contexts.

**Method:** Partner with three creative spaces (galleries, studios, co-working spaces) in London to host monthly "Creative Assembly" events—afternoon gatherings (2-6pm) combining creative workshops, music, and curated NA beverage service. Position NA drinks as enabling presence and creativity rather than replacing alcohol. Document attendee demographics, creative output, community formation, and repeat attendance.

**Success metric:** >80% capacity attendance by third event; >50% repeat attendees; measurable community formation (social media connections, collaborative projects initiated); attendee feedback indicating NA beverages enhanced rather than detracted from experience.
